## 1

Triton rose from the rocks at the cliff's edge and looked at the horizon. He was tall for seventeen and broad through the shoulders from hauling nets, and his hair was the gold of dry sand, and his eyes were blue. Far off, the molten rock marched toward his home. He could hear the cries of birds and creatures as the homes of the forest were consumed. There wasn't much time. He needed to protect his city, his people, his home.

Atlantis shone on the coastline below him. The city climbed the hillside out of the jungle in tiers, the old concrete houses whitewashed against the sun, the newer ones left gray, and between them the green of the trees that nobody had cut yet. The smithies were on the second tier, and their chimneys had stopped smoking, which he had never seen in daylight. Below them the artisan streets ran down to the harbor, and the harbor ran out into the sea on three long stone arms, and at the ends of the arms were the trading ports, where the wealth of the world came in and the fruits of the jungle went out. Twelve ships were tied up at the quays. One of them had a hull that caught the light and threw it back yellow. Golden cupolas topped the tallest buildings, a siren song to the seas of opportunity. On the far side of the city, where the North altar had stood, the cranes of the New World Center stood against the sky like the ribs of something dead.

His trident thrummed in his hands. It was the tool he knew best in the world; he had cleaned fish with the flat of it and pried mussels off the harbor stones with the tines and carried it on his shoulder up every path on the island. It was not that tool now. The flecks of rust were gone from the three points, and the points were gold, and barbed, and the shaft, which had been ash wood gone gray with salt, was silver and polished and shone in the sun. It weighed next to nothing. The power in it came up through the haft and into his arms like the charge in the air before a storm, and it wanted to be used.

He did not stop to look at it. He turned and ran.

The trees flew by him. His legs, which had been strong before, were something else now, and he went down the cliff path in strides that would have broken his ankles yesterday. His shoulders plowed through the brush and carved a path forward. The heat came behind him. The cries of the birds and the bellows of the beasts grew louder, and all around him the fire rained, red embers falling on his hair and going out. The forest canopy burned bright and lit the sky a red-orange hue. Smoke and heat clouded his vision but he knew these woods, and pressed on to the city.

He had prayed for rain a hundred times as a boy and gotten it twice. He had never prayed for this.

Triton reached the gates and crossed the threshold into the city. The molten rock had already ignited the outbuildings on the upper tier, and piles of cut logs smoldered in the yards and filled the air with flame and ash. The people rushed in a panicked frenzy, carrying children and whatever they could lift. A woman went past him with a birdcage. A man went past with a rolled carpet on his shoulder and nothing else.

"Get to the boats! We need to evacuate!" yelled a man as he hurried his family along. He had a loosely packed bag on his back, and he followed a woman down the road toward the sea and did not look up at the hill.

Triton moved with speed through the streets. Bystanders stopped to stare at the tall, muscled boy sprinting by them. He knew the city the way a boy knows his home, and he eased around the market stalls and the familiar corners, past the fish market where his father's stall had been, past the Temple of Poseidon with the merchant tables still crowding its steps and the silks on them beginning to smoke. He cut over a fence and through the New World Center construction site.

This would be easier if they hadn't torn down the North altar, thought Triton as he weaved around machines and ducked under cranes. The site was abandoned. The workers had fled the encroaching lava, and the foundation pit stood open, and in the bottom of it, half buried in gravel, were the cut stones of the old altar, waiting to be carted away.

He leapt the fence on the far side, barely touching the wire, and landed running, and went down the street toward the temple.

The wind whipped through the streets, carrying the embers onto the timber roofs, and the fire moved across the thatch of the old quarter faster than a man could run. The flames danced angrily in the air, ravaging the older buildings. A roof came down behind him. He did not turn around.

He reached the temple at the city's center. It had been the largest building in Atlantis once, and it was overshadowed now by the counting-houses on either side of it, and its walls were dingy with dirt, and the paint on its door was chipped and peeling. The reverence once reserved for the gods had gone somewhere else. Triton opened the doors wide, brought his hands together, bowed, and stepped inside.

The trident erupted in a bright blue light as soon as he crossed the threshold, illuminating the dark space within. The noise of the fleeing city vacuumed away as the door closed behind him. In the light, a hooded figure knelt by a candle at the altar.

The kneeling form whispered to himself, chanting. The blue light from the trident softened to a rippling blue and gold on the walls. Triton stepped closer, catching his breath. The candle flickered.

"You need to leave," said Triton. "The volcano erupted and the town is burning." He took another step closer. The chanting grew louder and more clear, but Triton couldn't understand the words.

"Get up. Leave!"

The hooded figure remained, chanting. The flame in front of him grew longer, brighter.

The hair on Triton's arms rose. He raised the trident and pointed it at the figure.

"I need you to leave. The gods will protect you, but you can't be here." His grip tightened. He extended the trident to prod the man.

The cloak collapsed the moment the tines touched it. There was nothing in it. The candle flickered bright for a moment, and went out.

A laugh came out of the dark, deep and rocky, and echoed across the waves of blue light that washed the walls.

"Ha. Ha. Hahhh. It's too late now, Triton. The idolators will be removed from this world, and those who remain will forever remember the Gods."

Triton spun around, sweeping the air with the trident, searching for the voice, and saw no one.

I only wanted them to remember, he thought.

With the voice gone the only sound came from the ceremonial pool bubbling on the altar. He advanced, taking cautious steps toward it. He had to seat the trident to save everyone. He did not know how he knew that. He knew it the way he knew the paths in the woods.

The pool was old stone, older than the temple around it, a basin cut in the floor as wide as a man's arms. The water in it was black and moving. He looked down into it and saw his own face come up out of the dark, streaked with ash, the hair singed at the temples, and his eyes. They were blue. There was something gold moving in them that had not been there yesterday.

He brought his hands together and bowed over the pool, the way the priests had taught him when he was small enough to be carried to the new-moon festivals. Then he turned the trident in his hands, points to the sky, and set the butt of it down into the water, and planted it on the stone of the basin, and held it there.

The water went still.

The floor moved under him. It did not shake the way the ground had been shaking all morning. It dropped, smoothly, the way a boat drops off the back of a swell, and kept dropping, and the walls of the temple groaned, and outside the sound of the city came back all at once and changed. The screaming went up. Under it was a sound he had heard every day of his life and never from this direction, which was the sea.

The doors were shut. He had shut them himself, and the sound had gone out of the room when they closed, and it did not come back now; he could not see the square or the harbor or the boats. He could only hear it. He heard the sea come over the harbor walls, a long low roar that was not a wave breaking but a wave that did not break, and come up the artisan streets and over the second tier, and he heard the screaming go up as it came, and then he heard the screaming stop, not fall away but stop, all at once, the way a voice stops when a hand covers a mouth. Then a sound like a great breath drawn in, and a hiss that was the fire meeting the water and the water winning, and then nothing. The groan of the temple walls under a weight that had not been there. And, through the shut doors, no light at all where the seams of them had shown a red line a moment ago — and then a light that was not the fire and was not the sun, a green light, coming up cold under the doors and along the floor toward his feet.

The trident stood in the pool and did not move, planted on the stone, and Triton held it standing, both hands on the haft. The water came in under the doors and over the threshold and rose up his legs, cold, and it was not like water. It went through him. He felt his own body let go of something it had held since he was born and take hold of something else, and he did not have words for either thing, and he did not let go of the haft.

The green light under the doors deepened as the city went down — green, and then blue, and then it was only a deep and even dark, and the roar was gone, and the screaming was gone, and the fire was gone, and it was quiet, and it was cool.

He would make sure they remembered. He would see to it himself.

## Part One — The Court of Gold

## 2

Ursula woke in the dark and lay still and listened for the light.

It came the way it always came, with no sound at all. The wall across from her bed was pressed coral, gray in the dark, and then it was not gray. A line of gold came up along the top of it where the wall met the ceiling, and widened, and slid down, and by the time it reached the floor the whole room was the color of the reef. The sun had reached the shallows. Somewhere a long way above her it was morning.

She was already at her father's desk before the gold reached the floor. She had been at it since she was six, and she was eighteen now, and the habit was in her arms. She took a sheet of pressed kelp from the stack and weighted the corners with two smooth stones and uncapped the ink. The ink was cuttle-black and smelled of iron. The pen was an urchin spine, ground to a point, and it had been her mother's.

Article Thirty-One, she wrote. Of Leases in Standing Kelp.

She knew the article. She could have written it with her eyes shut. She wrote it anyway, because the copying was not for the knowing, her father said; it was for the hands. A lease in standing kelp ran twelve seasons. The tribute was three spans in twelve, cut and bundled and delivered to the shelf-stair by the last day of the season, or four spans if delivered late, or the lease at the lessor's option. The lessee could not sublet. The lessee could not cut below the holdfast. The lessee's heirs took the lease at the lessee's death for the seasons remaining and no more.

Every one of the forty-one articles had been written by her mother. Ursula had not known that until she was ten. She had thought the Mercantile Code had always existed, like the reef.

"You've got the stones the wrong way round," her father said from the door.

She looked. The two stones on the corners of the sheet were the same stone, as far as she could tell. She said so.

"No," Odell said. He came in and settled to the floor, sinking down and coiling his heavy gray tail beneath him until he rested on it, which he did every morning, and which he did not do anywhere else, because he was a teacher of law and a court notary and a man of the third terrace, and men of the third terrace held themselves upright in a room and did not fold down to the sand like laborers at a rest. He was broad and gray and he moved slowly, and his eyes were dark and did not miss things. He picked up the two stones off the sheet, and the sheet curled at the corners, and he set the stones on the floor in front of him a hand apart. "This one is the lessor. This one is the lessee. Which is heavier?"

"They're the same."

"Are they?"

Ursula sank down across from him and coiled her tail under her the same way. They had been doing this since she was six. She had thought, at six, that it was a game about stones. She picked up the first stone. It was round and dark and worn smooth. She picked up the second. It was a little flatter.

"This one," she said, and held up the flat one.

"Why?"

"Because the lessor can end the lease if the tribute comes late. The lessee can't end anything. So the lessor's side has more in it."

"Good. So the deal is unfair."

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because—" She stopped. She had walked into it, and she had walked into it a hundred times before and would again, because that was how he taught. "Because the lessee gets twelve seasons of kelp he didn't have to plant. That's on his side. And the lessor gets three spans in twelve for kelp he didn't have to cut. That's on the other. And the ending clause is only there so the lessor's kelp doesn't rot in a bad lessee's hands. So it's not weight, it's—" She put the two stones down and moved them until they sat level with each other. "It's balance. Each side has something the other side wants, and something the other side can lose."

"A fair deal has two sides you can weigh," Odell said.

"You say that every morning."

"I'll say it every morning until I die, and then you'll say it." He reached into the pocket of his coat and took out a third stone and set it down between the other two. It was white. "What's this?"

She had not seen a third stone before. "I don't know."

"Guess."

"The tribute?"

"No. The tribute is inside the deal. This is outside it." He tapped it. "This is me. The notary. The witness. The seal. What does the third stone weigh?"

"Nothing," she said. "It doesn't give anything and it doesn't get anything. It just—" She looked at the three stones on the floor. "It sits there."

"It sits there," Odell agreed. "It keeps the other two from moving. That's all it does and that's everything it does. If either side wants to move a stone afterward, they have to move it past me. And I don't move." He looked at her over the stones with his dark eyes. "You understand? A seal isn't a party to the deal. A seal is a promise that the deal will still say tomorrow what it said today."

"And if the seal moves?"

"Then it isn't a deal anymore," her father said. "It's a taking."

He picked up the white stone and put it back in his pocket, and left the other two on the floor, and rose off the floor with a hand on the desk, and Ursula stayed down a moment longer, looking at the two stones, before she put them back on the corners of the sheet and finished Article Thirty-One.

Sebastian came at the second bell, the way he came every fifth day, and she heard him before she saw him, because he was singing.

He was a crab. He was red going brown at the edges of the shell, and he was not large, and he came in through the kitchen at the level of the floor and went up the leg of the table and onto the tabletop the way other guests came in through the front, and he did all of it singing, and he had a bundle of kelp-sheets tied with cord under one claw that was as big as he was.

"Ah! There she is! My favorite copyist in eleven seas!" He set the bundle down and made a bow that was mostly eyestalks. "Odell. You look terrible. It suits you."

"It's the light," said Odell.

"It's the hour, is what it is. Nobody with sense is awake at the second bell. I've been awake since the first." He rapped the bundle with a claw. "The cold-current counts. All twelve villages. A sum for every basket and a basket for every sum, and not one of them adds up, and by tonight they will. Ursula, my dear, if I ever tell you to go into accounts, strike me."

"You told me last month."

"Then I hope you struck me."

Ursula put a bowl in front of him. Her mother was already gone; her mother went to the counting-house before the light and came home after it, and had done so for three seasons now, and there was a bowl for her that had been put back on the shelf. Sebastian ate what was in his bowl without stopping talking, which was a skill she had never seen anyone else manage.

"I'm writing the festival song," he said. "Again. Do you know how many festival songs I have written? Two hundred and eleven. Do you know how many the king has asked me to write? Two hundred and eleven. The man has never once said, 'Sebastian, let's sing last year's.' A kingdom's two legs, I tell him. The song and the sum. You can't stand on one." He hummed something, and stopped, and hummed it differently, and stopped. "What do you think?"

"It's the same as the Nine Ships," Ursula said.

Sebastian looked at her with his eyestalks very still, and then laughed so hard he had to hold the edge of the bowl. "It's the same as the Nine Ships. Odell, she's got your ears and your mother's mouth and I don't know which one to be more afraid of. Yes. It's the same as the Nine Ships. Everything I write is the same as the Nine Ships, because the Nine Ships is the only song anyone has ever wanted to hear twice." He waved a claw. "Nine hulls opening on the reef in one night. The tenth let go to tell it. Try writing anything after that."

"How are the counts?" Odell asked.

"Terrible. I told you."

"You said they don't add up."

"They don't. They will." Sebastian went back to the bowl.

"Short, or long?"

Sebastian did not answer for a moment. Then he said, "Odell, my old friend, there are two kinds of numbers in this world, the ones that come to you and the ones that go from you, and the only thing anyone needs to know about a number is which kind it is. These are the kind that come. Be glad." He wiped his mouth with the back of a claw. "Now. Tomorrow. The survey."

"The Verge," Ursula said.

"The Verge. Clause nine and all the rest of it. Your survey master is a good man and a bad singer and he'll make you count feeding shelves until your eyes fall out, and you'll thank him for it when you're forty. Twelve of you. Last field year." He looked at Odell. "She'll be the best of them."

"She'll be the quietest of them," said Odell, "which is a different thing, and a better one."

Ursula looked at the bundle on the table. It was tied with the same cord as always and it was thicker than always, by about a third, and Sebastian had set it down with the knot toward himself, which he did not usually do.

She did not say anything about it. She noticed it, and put it where she put things, and got the bowls.

Alister came at the third bell, and did not sing, and did not need to.

He came to the front door, because the Marrens came to front doors, and he leaned on the frame of it, because the Marrens leaned, and he had gold wire worked into his hair in three thin braids and a torque of reef-gold at his throat that would have bought Odell's house. He was the same age as Ursula to the month. They had been assigned to the same cohort of court children at six, when the palace took the third terrace's clever ones and the first terrace's rich ones and put them in a room together and taught them the reef, and they had been friends since the first day, when he had shown her how to get out of the room.

"Scribe," he said.

"Merchant."

"Are you ready to count clams?"

"That's tomorrow."

"Today is the day Vell tells us how to count them, which is worse. Come on. He wants us in the charter room by the fourth bell and I'm not swimming it with your father's whole library on your back." He looked past her into the house and raised his voice. "Master Odell! Sebastian! I'm stealing her!"

"Bring her back with the same number of arms," Sebastian called from the table.

They went out into the reef.

The third terrace was the middle of the city and looked it. The houses were pressed coral and the streets were sand, and the gold was on the walls the way it was everywhere in Atlantica, but it was a thin gold on the third terrace, a wash, and on the second it was thicker, and on the first, up in the warm water where the Marrens lived and the light came down clean, the gold was in the walls and not on them, and the palace above the first terrace was not gilded at all because it was not made of anything else. Ursula had been inside it four times. She had been invisible each time, which was the correct way for the third terrace to be inside the palace, and she had seen a great deal.

"My father says the counts are late," Alister said. They were swimming the long way, along the shelf-stair, because he liked to be seen on it. "From the cold current. He says the king's furious."

"Sebastian has them."

"Sebastian has everything. That crab could tell you what I had for breakfast." He grinned. "He could tell you what the king had for breakfast, which is more useful. I'm going to be him one day, you know. Not a crab. In the room."

"You've said."

"I'll keep saying it until it's true. That's how it's done." He looked up. The light was coming down through the water in bars, and above the palace, where the reef broke the surface, it was white. "You know what the first terrace does with its clever children? It sends them to court. You know what the third terrace does? It sends them to the scriptorium. To copy the first terrace's contracts. That's the whole difference. Everything else is torques."

"The contracts are the whole city," Ursula said.

"The contracts are the whole city, and the city is owned by people who have never read them." He said it lightly. He said most things lightly. "That's your job, scribe. Read them. And when you find the one that says the Marrens own the third terrace, tell me first."

A work crew was going up the shelf-stair ahead of them, twenty laborers from the regions roped to a sledge of cut coral for the palace's new east wing, and they were singing to keep the stroke, and it was the founding song, because it was always the founding song. Ursula had heard it every festival of her life and it went through her the way water did.

We left the greed of men behind us, and the anger of the gods above—

Every child knew the story. The city had lost its way once, long ago, and the gods had been angry, and the king — chosen, with the trident in his hand — had brought his people down into the cool water and saved them, and left the greed of men behind, and built a golden reef where nothing could touch them. Two hundred years. She had never thought about it more than she thought about the light. It was the shape of the world.

"—and the king who does not die," the laborers sang, and one of them, a big man with a rope burn across his shoulders, sang the next line in a voice that was not the festival voice — "and the tribute that does not either" — and the men around him laughed, short, and the foreman shouted, and they went back to the words.

Alister had not heard it. He was looking at the palace.

Ursula had heard it. She noticed it, and put it where she put things.

In the charter room under the palace, Master Vell stood in front of twelve students with a face like a closed door and read them the Verge Charter, all twenty-two clauses of it, in a voice that did not go up or down, and made them read it back, and it was as boring as Alister had promised, and she loved it. Clause nine she had by heart before he reached it. The feeding shelves from the Needle to the Drop are held in common by the peoples of the Verge in perpetuity, tribute notwithstanding. Six words at the end that did all the work. Her mother had written those six words.

She came home after dark with her eyes aching from the small print and her father was at the desk and her mother was not home.

Odell did not say anything about it. He had stopped saying anything about it in the second season. Ursula ate, and copied Article Thirty-Two, which was Of Leases in Shellfish Beds and was longer, and the gold went off the walls the way it had come on, and the room was gray, and then it was dark, and she lit a lamp.

Ione came in at the seventh bell.

She was a tall woman and she carried herself straight, and she had taught commerce to the first terrace's children for thirty years and had written the code they traded under, and she did not look like a woman who had done any of that tonight. She looked like a woman who had been bent over a ledger a long time. She set her satchel by the door and hung her cloak on the hook and said, "There's a bowl," and Ursula said, "It's on the shelf," and Ione took it down and did not eat from it.

"How was Vell?" she asked.

"Long. He read us the whole charter. We map the shelves tomorrow, from the Needle to the Drop."

"Good. Clause nine."

"Clause nine."

Ione nodded. She held there with the bowl in her hands. Odell had turned from the desk to look at her, and he did not say anything, and after a moment he turned back.

"I'm going up," Ione said. "Don't stay at it too late."

She crossed the room and rose toward the upper floor of the house. As she went, Ursula looked up at her, the way she always did, to say goodnight — and her mother's eyes went to the lamp, and to the way up, and to the wall, and did not come to her.

Ursula stayed at the desk with the pen in her hand and the ink drying on it.

Then she put it down, and capped the ink, and set the two stones on the corners of the sheet, and rose after her.

## 3

They mapped the feeding shelves for six days, and on the sixth day Ursula and Alister slipped the survey.

It was easy to do and they had done it since they were nine. Master Vell counted heads at the Needle in the morning and again at the shelf-stair at dusk and not once between, because between was work, and work watched itself. So at the midday rest, when the ten others were eating in the lee of the Needle and Vell was marking his slate, Ursula and Alister took their measuring-lines and went over the lip of the Drop as if to sound the deep shelves, and kept going, along the face of the outer reef where the current ran cold and the coral changed from the fat gold shapes of the shallows to the thin hard fans of the Verge, and nobody could see them from the Needle, and nobody did.

"Vell will count eleven at dusk," Ursula said.

"Vell will count twelve. Petra owes me and she can throw her voice. You worry like a notary."

"I am going to be a notary."

"You're going to be the best notary," Alister said, "which is the saddest thing anyone has ever been," and he laughed and shot ahead of her along the reef face, and she went after him, and for a while it was just the two of them in the cold water with the reef going by, and no charter, and no counting, and the deep going down blue and then black on their left hand, and it was the best hour of the field year, and she would remember it as the best hour for a long time after there were no more good ones.

They came on the boy at the Verge feeding grounds, where the reef flattened into a broad shelf of shellfish beds, and there was a Verge family working the beds, and the boy was driving them off.

She knew what he was before she knew anything else about the scene, because she knew the charter. The family were Verge folk — reef people, not merfolk, the old intelligent kind that the charters called the peoples of the Verge and the first terrace called worse. There were four of them, an old one and two grown and a small one, and they were built low and broad for the beds, plated and slow, and they had baskets, and the baskets were half full of shellfish, and they were backing away from the beds with the baskets held against themselves.

The boy driving them was merfolk, and young, and wore a house-mark Ursula knew — one of the second-terrace houses, a shipping family, not first but reaching. He had a coral-tipped goad and he was not hitting them with it. He did not need to. He put it near the old one's face and the old one flinched back and the whole family flinched with it, off the beds, toward the drop.

"These are held," the boy was saying, in the bored voice of someone repeating a thing. "House Corran holds the lease on these beds as of the new season. You people can work the outer shelves. Go on. These are held."

"The beds are worked nine generations," the old one said. Its voice was deep and slow and came from somewhere in the plating. "We are of the Verge."

"You're on a Corran lease is what you are. Go on."

Ursula was moving before she had decided to. She heard Alister say her name behind her, low, the way you say a name to stop someone, and she did not stop.

She put herself between the goad and the old one. She was eighteen and thin and had no house-mark worth reading and no goad and nothing but what she knew, and what she knew was the charter.

"There's no Corran lease on these beds," she said.

The boy looked at her. "Who are you?"

"There can't be a Corran lease on these beds, because these beds can't be leased. The Verge feeding grounds from the Needle to the Drop are held in common by the peoples of the Verge in perpetuity. Verge Charter, clause nine. Tribute notwithstanding, which means it doesn't matter what House Corran paid or to whom. You can't lease common ground. Whoever sold your house that lease sold it something that doesn't exist."

She said it flat, the way her father read a clause, without heat, because heat was a thing the other side could weigh and a clause was not.

The boy's face did several things. "My house holds the paper."

"Then your house holds a bad paper. Take it to a notary. Any notary. It won't hold." She did not move from in front of the old one. "You can go and argue clause nine with the palace scriptorium, or you can drive four Verge folk off common ground in front of two court survey students, with your house-mark on, and let us be the ones who take that to a notary. Those are the two things you can do. Pick one."

It was not that she had convinced him. She could see that she had not convinced him of anything; he did not believe her about the charter and did not care about the charter. What she had done was make it complicated, and House Corran, reaching for the first terrace, could not afford complicated with a witness in it. She had put a cost on his side of the deal that had not been there a moment ago, and she watched him weigh it, and watched it come down against him.

He spat a word at her that men of the first terrace did not use and men reaching for it should not have, and he took his goad, and he went.

The Verge family did not thank her. The old one looked at her a long moment, with eyes set deep in the plating, dark, and did not miss things — she thought of her father, absurdly, for no reason but the eyes — and then it said, "You are the scholar's child," which she had not expected, and she did not get to ask how it knew, because it turned and took up its basket and led its family back down onto the beds, and did not look back. The small one looked back. It looked at her the whole way down until the plating of the others hid it.

Then Alister was at her shoulder, and his voice was low and fast and angry in a way she had almost never heard it.

"That was a Corran," he said. "Do you know whose beds those are going to be next season no matter what clause nine says? Do you know what a Corran can do to a scriptorium placement?"

"Clause nine says—"

"I know what clause nine says. Everyone who can read knows what clause nine says. That's not the same as it mattering." He got in front of her and made her stop. He was not angry at her, she saw; he was frightened for her, which in Alister came out as anger, and that was almost worse. "You made a Corran back down in front of witnesses. He is going to go home and find out who you are. He already knows I'm a Marren, which means he knows I'm nobody worth the trouble, and he's going to decide you're nobody either, and I need that to be true. Do you understand? For the next hour I need you to be nobody."

"He was driving them off their own beds."

"He was, and now he's not, and they'll be back tomorrow, and so will he, and next season House Corran will have those beds and there won't be witnesses, and the only difference your clause nine will have made is that a Corran knows your name." He let out a breath. "I'm going to go after him. I'm going to tell him you're a scriptorium mouse who reads too much and it won't happen again and there's nothing here worth a report. That's what I'm going to do, and it's going to work, because I'm good at it, and you're going to let me, and you're going to hate it."

"Yes," she said. "I'm going to hate it."

"I know." He almost smiled. "You always do."

He went after the Corran boy, and she watched him go, and she watched what he did, though she was too far to hear it — the way he came up alongside the boy easy and open-handed, the way he made himself smaller and the boy larger, the way a laugh went between them after a moment, the boy's laugh, and Alister's hand on the boy's shoulder. Smoothing it. Making the water flat. It would work. It was already working. And she understood, watching, that she and Alister had seen exactly the same thing happen on those beds, and that he was not wrong about any of what he had said, and that the difference between them was not what they knew. It was what they did with a wrong once they saw it. She had stepped in front of it. He had stepped around it, to keep them both safe, and he was right that it was safer, and she would rather have been struck with the goad than do it his way.

They did not fight about it again on the reef. They fought about it, quietly, all the way back to the shelf-stair at dusk, and by the shelf-stair they had stopped, because they always stopped, because he made her laugh at the last of it and she let him, and Petra had thrown her voice and Vell counted twelve.

"Same time tomorrow?" Alister said at the terrace stair, where he went up and she went along.

"You're going to be in the room one day," she said. "The king's room."

"I told you I am."

"I believe you now." She had not meant it to come out the way it came out, and she saw it land, saw him decide to take it as the compliment and not the other thing, because that too was what he was good at.

"Get some sleep, scribe," he said, and went up toward the gold.

But she had heard the thing under the thing, the way she had learned to from a crab at her mother's table, and what she had heard was this: that clause nine, which said the Verge feeding grounds were held in common in perpetuity, tribute notwithstanding, in six words her mother had written — was, this season, under review. The field year ended, and Ursula was placed in the scriptorium, and for a season the world was small and good and made of ink.

The scriptorium was on the third terrace, under the law courts, a long low room of copyists at long low desks, and the work was the work she had done at her father's desk since she was six, only there was more of it and it mattered. She copied leases and charters and the findings of the courts and the great tedious rolls of tribute, and she was good at it, faster than the copyists twice her age and cleaner, and she kept her head down and her voice low the way her father had taught her and the way Alister had told her to, and no Corran came. She began to think none would.

She did not stop reading what she copied. That was the one thing her mother had built into her that no amount of keeping her head down could reach. A copyist was not supposed to read; a copyist was a hand. But her mother had written the code, and her father had taught her that a clause was a live thing that could be moved, and so she read every word she copied, and she noticed things, and she put them where she put things.

What she noticed, that season, was the gold.

It was in the tribute rolls. She copied the cold-current counts in the winter — the same twelve villages whose sums Sebastian had carried tied in a bundle to her mother's table, a year ago and every year — and the sums came out, at the bottom, in gold. Not in kelp, not in shellfish, not in the labor-spans the villages paid in, all of which she understood and could weigh. In gold. A line at the foot of each village's roll, rendered to the crown treasury, and a figure, and the figure was larger than the village. She knew what the cold current produced. She had mapped its shelves. The gold at the bottom of the roll was more than the whole current could have made if it sold its own bones, and it appeared every year, and it balanced nothing she could find on any other page.

She said nothing about it in the scriptorium. She copied it, accurately, and brought it home in her head.

The king came to the east shallows before the light, at the turn of the season, as he always did, and set the trident against the coral, and the coral took the gold the way a sick man takes a meal.

He had no other way to say it to himself, and he had been saying it to himself for two hundred years. The reef did not refuse the gold. Nothing refused the trident. He laid the light along the branching heads and the gold went in and brightened, and the polyps closed against it, and the growing edge of the reef — the pale new fringe that any reef in any sea puts out in a season — sat under the gold like a hand held still under a coin. It took. It did not thrive. Three terraces of the finest coral in any ocean, and in two hundred years, left to itself, it had grown less than a wild reef grew in ten, and every season he came out before the light and fed it, and every season it needed feeding sooner.

The water in the east shallows was warm. It was always warm above the city; the gold held the sun's heat the way it held the light, and the warmth of Atlantica was one of the things the songs praised, the mild bright water where a child could swim in any season. Beyond the reef's shadow the sea ran colder, and the line between the two waters was sharp enough to feel on the back of the hand, and the line had moved outward again since last season. He noted it, and did not note it, in the way he had of keeping two sets of accounts.

He worked until the light came down from the surface and made his work invisible, gold into gold. Then he rested the trident across his shoulder and hung in the warm water and watched his city wake up.

It was, by any measure he knew, the finest thing in the sea. The terraces came out of the dark one ring at a time, and the lamps went out street by street as the sun took over, and the third terrace sent up its copyists and its clerks, and the second sent down its stall-keepers, and the shelf-stair filled and moved like a current given a purpose. Ten thousand people, fed, housed, at peace. No wall around any of it. No army in it. There had been no war in a hundred years, and there were men and women going down that stair to work who had never in three long lifetimes seen what the trident did, and would never have to.

That was the accounting he kept in the daylight, and it balanced.

Six daughters, he thought, watching the palace take the sun. Attina would be at the tall windows by now with the day's lists, because she was the eldest and grave about it. The little ones would still be asleep. Six daughters, and each one of them born in the mild water, in the peace, under the songs. Whatever else was in the ledger — and he kept, in the dark hours, a second ledger — that stood on the credit side, and it outweighed.

A dynasty dependent on the gift, something said in him, in a voice that was not quite his own thinking, and he put it away, the way he had learned to put things away.

He went in to the day's table.

The paper was one sheet, folded twice, still holding the shape of the inside of a coat.

Two paragraphs, copied out in the broad patient hand he had watched attest half the instruments of his reign, set side by side with a ruled line between them, the way a teacher sets out a lesson. No heading. No names. No question. The seventh article of the settlement, twice.

He read it twice.

There was no one in the small hall but the crab on the table and the lamp between them. The palace above was asleep. Somewhere below, the night current was moving through the streets of the third terrace, and the lamps of the counting-house were out, and ten thousand people were asleep in the mild warm water, under the songs.

He had moved the article on a winter night ninety-one years ago, alone, in the archive, with the court's counterpart flat on this same table. A bad season; the Isles' boats thick on the northern banks that the settlement had given away in his grandfather-envoy's time — given cheap, given badly, given by a king one war old who had not yet understood that everything he conceded was conceded forever. He had stood over the page half the night, and then he had done the thing only he could do, and it had taken no effort at all. That was the part he had never told anyone and never would: how little it weighed. Stone, water, fire, a fleet's keels, a line of ink a hundred years dry — the trident moved matter, and words on a page were matter, and the trident did not know what a promise was. The knowing was supposed to be his job.

He had accounted for the humans' counterpart; the humans could hold whatever paper they liked, at the bottom of whatever archive, under whatever seal — their paper against his patrols. He had accounted for the court's scribes, who copied what was in front of them. "There is no record," the registrar said, each word set down separately, like weights, and he held her eyes while he said it, and that was the most anyone did for her in any office of Atlantica: one man, briefly, letting her see that he knew what sentence he was saying.

The summons, from memory, engrossed phrase by engrossed phrase. The three petitions and their three fates. No bodies. No trial. No record.

Not an accusation — an absence with edges. A trial could be argued with. A record could be collated. A body could be wept over. What had been done was the removal of every handle by which a fact can be held: nothing to appeal, nothing to disprove, nothing to bury. Her parents had not been killed, as far as any page in Atlantica would ever testify. They had been unentered. She had come home on the third evening to a house that was exactly as she had left it, except clean. The study straightened. The stacks squared. She did not blame him. That was the frightening discovery of those days, the one that told her what she was becoming: she could not find the blame. He had smoothed it over. He always had; she had watched him do it since they were nine, and loved him alongside the knowledge the way you love a river alongside the knowledge that it drowns people. Everyone in the city had weighed the same two stones — the truth of two old scholars against the peace of the golden reef — and everyone had reached the same sum, the registrar and the gate-clerk and the neighbors with their covered bowls and the oldest friend her family had, and the sum was the song. It was not malice. It was arithmetic. A city was a ledger, and her parents had become a number that went from you, and the books do not argue with the season. Somewhere above all of it sat the one hand that had actually moved — ink doesn't move; men move it — but the hand had needed a thousand small clerks to square the page after, and the clerks had squared it singing.

A fair deal has two sides you can weigh. All her life it had sounded like the founding law of the world. Her heart went through her like a spear-haft, and then the shape of the voice reached her a half-beat behind the sound of it, the way it does with a voice you have known since you were six, and she came down the last turn and there was Alister, in the dark, at the foot of the stair that went nowhere, out of breath.

He had come fast and dressed badly for it — festival-fine, a first-terrace dinner still on him, the gold wire in his hair catching what little light there was, and she took the whole of him in and understood the shape of the evening she had not been at: the long table, the men his father's age, the thing said over the wine, low, that was not meant for the son of the house but reached him anyway. He had left a dinner and swum the dark side of the city in his good clothes. His chest was still heaving with it.

"How did you know I'd be here," she said.

"I didn't. I went to the house and the window was open and the current through it was full of—" his hand moved, helpless, describing it, "—paper. Little pieces of paper, going by in the dark, like snow going up. So I knew you knew. And then I thought about who would have told you, and what route a very careful person would pick, and I've known you nineteen years, Ursula, there was only ever going to be one stair." He said it fast, the way he said things when he was frightened and being light about it. Then he stopped being light. "There's an order. I heard it tonight, at my father's table, from a man who signed it. I came to tell you to run and you're already running, so."

"So," she said.

They looked at each other in the dark at the bottom of the stair, at the edge of the last coral, and the whole nineteen years stood there with them — the schoolroom and the way out of it, the outer reef, the goad and the clause, the bell tower, the two stones sitting warm in her pockets — and she watched him gather himself to say something, and she knew every path a sentence could take through Alister Marren, she had mapped him the way she mapped shelves, and she watched him stand at the head of all of those paths, and she saw the moment he chose, and her heart broke before he said it, because she saw which one it was.

"I'm sorry," Alister said.

That was the sentence. It was even true; that was the worst thing about it; it came out of him with his whole chest behind it, the truest thing he had ever said to her. I'm sorry. Not — this is wrong. Not — I asked, and I'll keep asking, and let them come for me too. Not — even now, even at the foot of the last stair with an order signed and the dark waiting — come with me, we'll go to my father, we'll fight it, I will spend what I am to keep you. Every one of those sentences existed. She could hear all of them, standing there, the way you can hear a chord in the note that was actually played. He was sorry. He would grieve her, truly, the way the city grieved — beautifully, and in tune, and without asking any questions — and he would be at the counting of whatever came next, because the counting was where he had always been going, and there was no version of Alister that was not in the room, and no version of the room, now, with her in it.

And the thing she understood, in that moment at the foot of the stair — the thing that would sit in her for thirty years, colder and heavier than anything Sebastian had not said through the window — was that she was not even surprised. She had watched him choose the room over the wrong at nine years old on the outer reef, and she had told herself it was one afternoon, and it had never been one afternoon. It was him. It was the whole of him; she had seen it the first day and loved him anyway and called the seeing unkindness; and now the same choosing stood in front of her in festival clothes saying sorry with its whole honest heart, and she could not even hate it, because he had come. The order was signed and he had come, in his good clothes, at real risk, fast enough to be out of breath — come all this way, at last, to be sorry. It was so much more than the city had done. It was so much less than the least of what she needed. Both of those things were true at once and they did not balance, and nothing between the two of them was ever going to balance now, and she was the only one of the two of them who could weigh, so she was the only one who would ever know it.

"I know you are," she said.

He flinched, and she watched him take it, and take it as his due, which he could always be counted on to do; his accounts were honest even when nothing else was. Then he said, quietly, in a voice nineteen years old: "Where will you go?"

"Down."

"There's nothing down there. Ursula. There's nothing—"

"There's nothing up here," she said, and it came out without heat, flat, the way you read a clause, and she saw it land on him harder than anger would have. She looked at him one more time — the gold wire, the fine coat, the face she had known longer than any face still living that would speak to her — and she did the accounting, because she was her mother's daughter too, and the accounting was this: he had given her the schoolroom door, and the reef, and the bell tower, and one night of stones bought at triple their worth, and it had all been real. And he was going to swim back up this stair to the lamps and the room and the rest of his life, and be kind, and be sorry, and rise. Both columns true. The books do not argue with the season.

"Goodbye, Alister," she said.

She went past him, off the last of the coral, out over the sand, south, and he did not follow, and she did not look back, and the not looking back was so exactly her mother that she almost laughed, and then she was crying, swimming hard and level and crying without sound in the dark, which — she made the entry in some flat clerk's corner of herself even as she did it — cost nothing extra, down here, where it was already salt.

Past the Needle the bottom ran cold and pale under starlight, and then there was no starlight, and she followed the shelf line by the feel of the water alone.

The cold came up through her in stages, like a tide filling a house. First the skin, which was nothing; a copyist's hands knew cold. Then deeper, into the muscle of the tail, a stiffness she had to swim against, and then a strange band of warmth as her body burned what it had, and then the cold again, closer to the center, patient. The taste of the water changed as she went — the bright mineral taste of the reef fading, and under it something older, iron and stone and dark, the taste of water that had never once been through a lamp-lit street, water that had been down here since before the songs, minding its own affairs.

She felt the edge before she saw it, because there was nothing to see. The shelf had been narrowing under her for an hour, sand to gravel to bare old rock, and then the feel of the water opened — the particular pressure of ground beneath you, which you never notice until it is gone, was gone — and she checked, and hung there, working her fins against the dark, at the lip of it.

The Drop. She had mapped its upper edge at eighteen, from a safe surveyor's distance, sounding it and entering the soundings and thinking nothing at all about the numbers except whether they were accurate. The numbers had been accurate. They had also been the depth of the whole world, and no line on a chart had said so.

Below her the water went down black, and blacker, and then some color that black was the doorway to, and it was cold coming up out of it, a cold with pressure in it, a cold that stood in the water like a wall stands in the air, and every animal length of her body said what it had been built across ten thousand generations to say at this exact lip: no.

She thought: the patrols don't come here because it goes nowhere.

She thought: nowhere is the only place they don't govern.

She thought — and the thoughts came faster now, cascading, the way they came when a thing was too big to think in order — Who signed the order? The same hand that signed the summons? Did anything sign it — is there a record of me, one line, somewhere, or am I already like them, unentered, a rumor with a lament coming? Was Sebastian lying then, at the counting-house, or lying now with the window and the stair — is this mercy or is this the tidy ending, the daughter lost at the shelf-edge, grief-swum into the dark, no bodies, no trial, no record? Is anyone behind me? Is that current or a wake? If I go down and the cold takes me, who will ever collate it? Who is left, in the whole golden city, who can even read?

— and she took a breath of the black iron water, and put a hand in each pocket, and closed each hand around a stone.

One round and dark. One a little flatter. Two sides you can weigh. Up there: the lamps, the songs, the room behind the door, an order with her name on it or no name at all, and everyone she had ever loved either unentered or singing or sorry. Down there: nothing, said everyone who had never been.

She weighed it. It was the first deal she had ever struck with no notary in the world to hold it, and the two sides came level, and then past level, and she felt them go.

Ursula tipped over the edge of the world and went down.

The pressure took her by the ears first, then the chest — a great slow hand closing, testing what it held — and she equalized the way divers of the Verge had taught the survey cohort, jaw and throat, again, again, falling. The last of the light did not fade the way evening fades. It thinned, like ink in water, up there above her tail, a grayness going gray, and then it was a memory of grayness, and then she could not have said, hanging in the black with her heart very loud, whether her eyes were open or shut, and it did not matter, and that was the fact about the dark that no chart had entered: past a certain depth, it is not the absence of anything. It is a place. It holds you the way water holds you. Something in it took her weight.

She fell for a long time. The cold stopped being cold at some point and became simply where she lived now, and the pressure stopped being a hand and became a garment, and her thoughts got slow and wide and very clear, the way they were in the last hour of a long night of copying, and she was beginning to wonder, with a copyist's detachment, whether this was what the end of the account felt like — whether she was already dead and just falling, and whether anyone would ever enter it, and under what heading —

— when she saw, far below her, and to the south, faint as a misremembered word, a glow.

Not the reef. She knew every light the reef made, the gold of the walls and the white of the surf-line and the strung colors of festival, and it was none of them. It was green, and it was moving — many small greens, drifting, wheeling slowly around a darkness at their center, patient as stars — and it was not the sun and it was not the trident, and those were the only two lights that had ever lit anything in the world of the songs.

Somebody down there had made their own.

She swam toward it.

## Part Two — Down Here

## 11

The glow was a town.

She had no other word for it, and for a long time, hanging at the edge of the light with the cold going through her in waves, she could not make the word fit what she was seeing. A town was streets and stalls and lamps on kelp-cord. What lay below her was a field of black stone chimneys, dozens of them, tall as the bell tower, and out of their throats poured smoke that was not smoke — it shimmered, silver-black, like heat over a festival griddle — and the water around them was warm. She could feel it from a hundred lengths away, rolling up at her through the dead cold, mineral and sulfurous and alive, and everything that lived in the Abyss, it seemed, had gathered to that warmth the way the city gathered to the festival: drifting rings of small green lights that were animals, curtains of blue that were animals, a slow constellation wheeling around the smokers' darkness, grazing, hunting, hanging, being.

Nothing in it was anything she knew. That was the first true fact of her new life, and it kept being the first fact for days. She had grown up on a reef that the songs called the garden of the world, and she had eighteen charts' worth of its creatures by heart, and not one thing in this warm dark had a name she had ever copied. Things went by her trailing light the way fine ladies trailed festival ribbon. Things watched her out of the stone with eyes the size of bowls, patient as debt. A fish swam past her — she decided it was a fish — carrying its own lamp on a stalk before it, angling the light down at the stone the way a clerk angles a lamp at a ledger, and she watched it go with her heart hammering cold in her chest and thought, ridiculously, of Sebastian: nobody with sense is awake at this bell.

Nobody welcomed her. Nobody drove her off. That was the second fact, and it took longer to learn, because she kept waiting for the one or the other — for the challenge, the charter, the who are you and under whose leave — and it never came. Things looked at her, assessed her length and her strangeness and her empty hands, and went back to their business. Down here, she would understand later, arrival was not an event. Everything in the Abyss had arrived once. It was a place made entirely of things that had come from somewhere else and been asked no questions, because the only question the dark had ever asked anybody was whether they could live in it.

She nearly couldn't.

The cold was the enemy the first season — the cold, and the arithmetic of warmth. The smokers' field was warm, but the warmth was owned, in the wordless way things were owned down here: the best water, the steady shelves in the smoke-shimmer where the heat came through the stone like a floor in the sun, all of it was held, generation on generation, by the things that had held it longest, and what was left for a newcomer was the margin — the flickering edge where warm met cold and went back and forth by the hour. She learned the margin. She learned to read the smokers the way she had read tide-tables, which chimneys ran steady and which ones stuttered, and to sleep in the lee of a stutterer when it woke, and to move before it slept. She learned that her body could take the cold in shifts, so many hours out, so many back; she learned the number for each and kept to it, and once, badly, she learned what happened one hour past the number, when the shivering stopped — the shivering stopping was the danger, not the shivering — and the water turned kind and wide and she began, very peacefully, to forget what she was for.

What saved her, that hour, she could never afterward set down in any record, and she tried more than once.

She had drifted off the margin into the open dark, past the last of the lights, and the cold had closed over her mind like a lid, and somewhere in the wide kind blur of it she had felt — this was the part that would not go into words — held. Not seized. Held. The way the water holds you, but chosen; the way a room full of your own people holds you, but empty. The dark of the Abyss, which every chart called the absence of everything, had gathered itself around her like a held breath, and something vast and patient and utterly without light had considered her, the whole length of her, the cold in her and the small stubborn heat, and had — kept her. She came back to herself against a stone she had no memory of reaching, in water just warm enough, with the certain knowledge that she had been carried, and the equally certain knowledge that nothing had touched her. Above her in the black, briefly, she thought she saw the dark be darker, the way a shape is darker than a shadow, and then it was only the Abyss again.

She told no one, because there was no one to tell. But she noticed that the old things of the deep, the plated and the eyeless and the ones like drifting cathedrals, all did the same small thing at the same hour of the long no-day — a kind of settling, a lowering, a moment of stillness aimed at nothing — and that nobody ever named the dark itself, in any of the click-and-drum languages she was learning; they said down here the way her city had said the crown, and left the subject standing courteously unaddressed, and after a while she did the same.

@· · ·

The cavern was in the margin, a fault in the old lava with walls at her back and one mouth she could watch, and it was nobody's, and then it was hers.

She had been in it nine sleeps when she began to write.

There was no ink in the world, down here. No pressed kelp, no pens; her mother's pen stood in a jar on a desk at the bottom of her old life. What there was, was stone — the cavern's walls were sheeted with a soft black glass that the smokers laid down, and it took a mark the way wax took a seal — and there were the spines of the dead urchins of the deep, which were longer than her hand and harder than any spine of the reef, and needed only grinding. She ground one against the wall for most of a sleep, and tested the point on her thumb, copyist-fashion, without thinking, and the familiarity of the gesture in that unfamiliar dark went through her like grief, and she gave herself the length of it, one breath, two. Then she set the point to the wall.

She did not write a contract. There was no counterparty in her life; there was nothing left in the world with two sides to weigh. She wrote the record.

@In the twenty-fifth year of my age, being the daughter of Odell the notary and Ione the teacher of commerce, scholars of the third terrace of Atlantica, I set down what I know, that it may be somewhere.

She wrote the summons, engrossed phrase by engrossed phrase, from the place where she put things. She wrote the gate, the four mornings, the changed answer. She wrote the three petitions and their three endorsements, and the registrar's sentence, each word set down separately, like weights. She wrote the song — the whole lament, every lovely lying verse of it, because the song was evidence too, the finest evidence there was: this is how it is done, this is the sound a record makes being erased. She wrote what her mother had found and how she had found it, heading by heading. And then, on the smoothest sheet of wall, in her best hand, with a straightedge of split slate, she wrote the seventh article of the settlement between the Crown of the Sea and the Crown of the Isles, twice over, side by side, word for word for word, the true text and the moved one, from memory, whole.

It took her the better part of a season. Her hand did not shake and her memory did not fail, because the memory was the one inheritance the crown had not known to seize, and when it was done she ground the spine fresh and cut one more line beneath the whole of it, deep, in letters as plain as her father's:

@No bodies. No trial. No record. This is the record.

@· · ·

The eels came during the writing. Afterward she could never say which sleep; they arrived the way the tide arrives, by having been arriving for a while.

She heard them first — or heard herself heard: the small dry scratch, scratch of the spine on the glass wall going out into the black water of the cavern mouth, and the black water listening. Then two long pale shapes at the edge of what the wall's faint vent-glow could reach, side by side, utterly still, watching her the way the deep watched everything, without appetite and without mercy, on file. She stopped. They stayed. She wrote again; they came a length closer. In this way, over many sleeps, the way a stray learns a doorway, they came in: two eels, green-pale, long as she was, one with a torn fin healed crooked and one without, and they took up two stations in her cavern as if the stations had been assigned them before the water was made — the crooked one high in the fault where the warm current pooled, the straight one low by the mouth, facing out.

They did not speak, then or ever. She never learned whether they couldn't or didn't; the Abyss was full of both kinds, and it was considered — she was absorbing the deep's one big manner without noticing — ill-bred to wonder. They were simply there, and then they were hers, or she was theirs. The crooked one was Flotsam and the straight one was Jetsam by the end of the season, because a scriptorium child alone in the dark will name things, and the names were a joke about wreckage that nobody would ever be alive in the right language to laugh at, and the first time she said them aloud both eels turned their heads, together, like a door opening, and that was that.

They watched her write for a hundred sleeps. And on some sleep in the middle of the hundred — she did enter this one, low on the wall, in small letters, because it seemed to her even then to be a fact of a new kind, the first credit entry in a ledger that had held nothing but debits since the festival — she woke from a cold dream of a bright street to find that she had been moved, in her sleep, gently, out of the path of the stutterer's chill and into the warm fault, and that the crooked eel was lying along her spine like a drawn line, and the straight one lay across the cavern mouth, facing out, in the manner of a thing that intends to be asked a question first.

They keep watch, she wrote under it. She looked at the sentence a while. Then, because the record was the record and she had sworn it nothing but the truth, she added: Over me.

@· · ·

The food began after the writing was known.

She had been surviving on the margin's gleanings — the pale grazers of the smoker-fields, the ribbon-weed that grew in the warm seams, all of it tasting of iron and sulfur and none of it enough — and she was thin, and getting slowly thinner, on the wrong side of an arithmetic she kept precisely and told no one. Word of the writing had gone out from her cavern by then, in the sideways way word moved down here — the eels came and went; things paused at her cavern mouth, sometimes, for the length of a look, and drifted on — and she had noticed the pauses getting longer, and the lookers stranger: the plated, the eyeless, the old. The Abyss coming, one by one, without ever saying so, to look at the wall where somebody was keeping an account of what the light had done.

She woke one sleep and there was food at the cavern mouth.

It lay on a flat stone that had not been there either — placed, both of them, stone and offering, just inside the line where her small warmth met the dark, like something set down at a border by a party that respects borders. It was meat, pale and clean and strange, of no animal she could name, prepared in no way she recognized, by hands or arms or mouths she could not begin to picture. It might have been anything. It was, by the standing law of every childhood story the reef had ever told her about the deep, precisely the thing you did not eat: the gift of the dark, the fare of the unnamed, the morsel that binds.

She was twenty-five years old, and an orphan of a city that sang, and thin, and watched over by eels, and hungry in a place that had asked her no questions.

She ate it without asking what it was.

## 12

They brought her the dispute at the turning of the season, and they brought it the way everything came down here: sideways, without announcement, in the manner of a thing that had decided to happen some time ago and was only now arriving.

She woke to find her cavern mouth occupied. On the left of it, low, against the stone, sat a Brooder — one of the plated diggers of the smoker-fields, broad as a door, patient as a door, its dozen young tucked under its skirts with only their eyes showing — and on the right, high, hung the Shoal, a hundred small lamp-fish schooled into one shape, which was the way the Shoal did everything, including argue. Between them, exactly between them, lay a flat stone with nothing on it. She would learn, in the years ahead, what the empty stone meant in the deep's grammar of borders: we bring a thing that is not food and not weapons. We bring a matter.

Jetsam, at the cavern mouth, had let them stay. That was the first ruling of the day, and it had not been hers.

The matter was the Old Stutterer.

It was the third chimney of the near field, the one whose habits she had learned in her first bad season — a smoker that ran hot for a stretch of sleeps and then failed, without pattern anyone had found, and slept cold, and woke again. Along its western foot ran a shelf of laid stone where the warmth came through like a floor in the sun, and the shelf was the finest brooding ground in the near field when the chimney ran, and the finest lamp-graze when it slept, because the grazing bloom came up in the cool. The Brooders had held the shelf time out of mind. The Shoal had grazed it time out of mind. Both of these things were true, because the chimney itself had divided the time between them, hot and cold, and no one had ever needed to say so.

Then the Old Stutterer had changed. For a year now it had run longer and slept less — she had noted it herself, in the margin-arithmetic of her first season, without wondering who else's arithmetic it was wrecking — and the Brooders' good seasons had stretched, and the Shoal's had shrunk, and the Shoal had begun grazing the shelf's edge while the chimney ran, in the margins, in the flicker, and one of the Brooder's young had struck at the lights, as Brooders' young do, and taken a mouthful of the Shoal — which was, depending on your grammar, either lunch or murder. In Atlantica it would have gone to the courts. Down here it had gone the way such things went: a feud, laid down like a keel, ready to be built on for a hundred years, the Shoal salting the Brooder's graze, the Brooder's kin walling the Shoal out of three more shelves, everyone's margin thinner every season, on and on, patient as everything down here was patient, until one party or the other was starved out of the field their great-grandkin had warmed.

They brought it to her because of the wall.

She understood that from the way the Brooder's eyes, all the long silence of that first audience, rested not on her but past her, on the sheeted glass where the record stood — the terms, the petitions, the two articles side by side in her best hand. Most of the deep could not read the reef's letters. It did not matter. Word had gone out, sideways, in the click-and-drum, in the way of the ignored everywhere, who keep better accounts of the powerful than the powerful keep of themselves: the one in the fault writes down what was done. Both sides of it. The done-to and the doers, in the same hand, on the same wall, weighed. The Abyss had no throne and no law but reciprocity, and it had looked at her wall for a hundred sleeps, one drifting pauser at a time, and concluded — without a meeting, without a word, in the manner of the dark deciding anything — that the field now contained someone who thought about fairness, the way it contained things that thought about currents or poison: a capability, washed down from above, possibly useful.

So she was not asked to judge. Judging was a throne's trick; the deep had watched thrones judge. The empty stone asked something older. Weigh this.

She heard both sides. It took three sleeps, because the Shoal could only speak at dusk-turn when it schooled dense enough to drum, and because the Brooder said one sentence per sleep, laying each down like a slab: The shelf is ours when it is warm. It has always been so. The warm is longer now. She listened the way her father had taught her — the party that talks longest is showing you their weak plank; the party that talks least is standing on theirs — and she went out and lay on the shelf herself through a full run-and-sleep of the chimney with Flotsam coiled beside her, feeling the heat come and go through the stone, and she asked the Brooder one question — when the chimney sleeps and the bloom comes, what do your young eat? — and the answer was: the bloom. Brooder young grazed the same cool bloom the Shoal did; the shelf in its cold season fed both; it was only the warm season the Brooders needed whole, and only the cold the Shoal could use at all.

And there it was, lying open like a bivalve: not one ground, two grounds, sharing a stone — a warm shelf and a cold shelf that happened to stand in the same place at different hours, disputed only because the language of place had no word for when. Her mother's voice, over a lease in standing kelp: most quarrels are two people holding different clauses of the same sensible deal, and no one has drafted the deal.

She drafted the deal.

She ground her spine-point fresh, and on the wall of the cavern, below the record, in letters large and plain enough to be read by lamplight and described by drum, she wrote two sentences:

@While the Old Stutterer runs, the west shelf is the Brooders', to the last stone the warmth reaches; while it sleeps, the shelf is the Shoal's, and the bloom on it.

@The chimney keeps the time; neither party keeps it; and whichever party the chimney favors in a long year owes the other the margin, in graze or in warding, at the season's turn.

Then she did the thing that she had been turning over for three sleeps, the thing that had no precedent in any court she had copied for, because every court she had copied for had a crown behind it and she had a wall. She could not enforce a word of it. There was no notary alive down here but her, and a notary, her father had said, is a promise that the deal will say tomorrow what it said today — but his promise had had the courts behind it, and the courts the crown, and the crown the trident. Hers had eels. So the deal would have to hold itself up, and she had drafted it to: the chimney kept the time, and the chimney could not be bribed; the margin-debt squared the long years, so neither side could be slowly robbed by luck; there was nothing in it to cheat that the cheating would not immediately cost. It was, though she did not think of it in these words until much later, the first thing she had ever built to stand with no one holding it.

"It needs your marks," she said. "Both. That's all a mark is — it says you stood here and called it level. It doesn't make you keep it. Nothing makes you keep it. It only makes sure that if one of you breaks it, everyone who can read this wall, and everyone they drum it to, will know exactly what was broken, and by whom, and what level looked like before you broke it." She looked from the plated eyes to the hundred small ones. "Down here I'm told that's called nothing. Where I come from it was called the whole world, and I watched it die of one moved line, so I'll tell you what I know for certain about marks on walls: they are worth nothing at all, right up until they are the only thing left. Mark it or don't."

The Brooder came forward first, hauling its skirts and its watching young, and pressed one armored knuckle into the soft glass below the sentences, a deep clean print. The Shoal descended all together, schooled tight, and did the astonishing thing she would tell the eels about for years as if the eels had not been present: it made itself into a shape — a slow spiral, tightening — and pressed the shape against the wall in a wheeling of a hundred small bodies, so that a hundred fin-tips stippled the glass in one unbroken curl, a signature made of everyone.

And it was done, and they went, the Brooder low, the Shoal high, out of the vent-glow, and the empty stone stayed at her cavern mouth — which meant, she would learn, the matter is lodged; the stone will come back if the matter does — and Ursula hung in her cavern looking at the two marks under the two sentences and felt something she had no entry for, light and terrible at once, like the first cold of the margin: the knowledge that it would probably hold, and that if it held it would hold because it was balanced, and for no other reason in the world, because there was no other reason in the world, not down here; the last court had been left up in the light with everything else, and balance held or nothing did.

Behind her, Flotsam, who had watched the whole solemnity from the warm fault with his crooked fin folded, uncoiled, drifted down the wall to the fresh marks, considered them for a long moment with the gravity of a chief clerk — and pressed his face into the glass beside the Brooder's knuckle-print, hard, twice, leaving two soft absurd smudges, nose and nose. Then he looked at her.

The sound that came out of Ursula startled the Shoal-light at the cavern mouth into a flicker; it startled Jetsam clean off his station; most of all it startled her. It was rusty and cracked and ran on longer than it should have, and it echoed off the record wall in a place where no one had made that sound possibly ever, in the whole history of the water: she was laughing. She had not laughed since the festival. She had assumed, in the flat entered way she assumed things now, that the laughing was one more item on the seized inventory — parents, house, name, city — and here it was, alive in her chest in the black bottom of the world, brought back out of her by an eel with a crooked fin signing a treaty with his face.

"That's not a valid mark," she told him, when she could. "You weren't a party."

Flotsam looked at the wall, and at her, and pressed his face into the glass a third time, deliberately, next to the first two — establishing, as it were, a right of way — and stationed himself under his own smudges with an air of appeal it, and she was still laughing, hoarse, unpracticed, in the dark, and that was the sleep she stopped keeping count of her exile in days.

Word moved, after. Fast, and sideways.

The empty stone came back in a season, with a different matter behind it — a water-rights tangle between two hunting ranges, thin margins, old blood — and after that a third matter, and a fifth, and the flat stones began to arrive faster than the matters could be walked, so that there was, for the first time, a waiting at her cavern mouth, which Jetsam regulated with the impartial ferocity of a gate-clerk. The wall grew its second section: below the record, the registry. Two sentences here, three there, a knuckle-print, a spiral of fin-tips, the strange delicate tooth-marks of things she was proud to have kept her face still on first meeting. Nothing on it could compel anyone to anything. Everything on it held, or mostly held, or held long enough to be redrafted level — because she took nothing across her stone that she could not make balance, and turned away what couldn't with both sides of the refusal written out, so that even her no had two columns.

It was at the fourth matter, or the fifth — a whole gathering, that one, both parties large and much-related, her cavern mouth crowded three deep with lights and plates and eyes — that she saw, near the back, at the edge of the vent-glow, a shape she knew.

It took her a moment, because the knowing was in the wrong place — it came up from the survey charts of her eighteenth year, from cold bright water a world above this one, from an afternoon of goads and clauses. Plated, low, broad-built for the beds. Young when she had seen it last; grown now, and travel-worn, the plates scarred at the edges the way they scarred when a thing had been moved on, and moved on, and moved on. A Verge creature. The small one, who had looked back at her the whole way down the beds while its family's baskets were held against the plating; looked back at the strange thin court girl who had put herself in front of the goad and made the wrong thing complicated.

It was watching her now across the crowded cavern mouth with those same deep-set eyes, and she understood, in the sideways way the deep taught you to understand things, everything the scars meant: the beds lost after all, next season or the one after, clause nine notwithstanding, when the witnesses were gone and the review was done; the family pushed down the shelf, and down, and down, one charter at a time — she had mapped, at eighteen, the exact ground it had been pushed from — until down ran out of shelf, and here it was, at the bottom of everything, at the back of a crowd at a witch's door. No. Not a witch, not yet; at the back of a crowd at a notary's door. Watching the scholar's child weigh things, in the dark, for the ones the light had finished with.

Neither of them made it a scene. She held its eyes the length of one breath, the way you acknowledge a creditor across a crowded room — because that is what it was; the whole reckoning of her old life stood at the back of that crowd wearing scars she had failed to prevent, and both of them knew it, and both of them knew that the failing had not been hers to help — and it lowered its head, once, slowly, the smallest possible motion of the heaviest possible thing, and she inclined hers, and then the Shoal-drum started up front and the matter at hand resumed, and the matter at hand was, as always, the whole of the law.

It stayed, after. It never said what it had come to say, that gathering or ever; it simply began, in the way of the deep, to be there — at the edge of hearings, at the heavy end of tasks, at the cavern mouth in the bad hours of the stutter-season, one more station in the small strange household that was assembling itself around her without a single word of its terms ever being drafted: two eels, a scarred digger of the Verge, the empty stones coming and going, the dark keeping its unnamed watch over all of it.

She was not happy. She would have corrected anyone who entered it so, flatly, citing the record: the dead were still dead, the songs still sung, the drawer still empty, the account still open. She was not happy.

But she was — for the first time since a lamp burned late in a counting-house window — home.

## 13

The Abyss had a song, and it took her four years to be allowed to understand it.

She had heard it from her first season — everyone heard it; it was the weather of the deep — but hearing it was not the same as being sung it. It moved through the smoker-fields the way the founding song had moved through the terraces, rising out of everywhere and nowhere, in the drum and click and long bone-hum of a dozen languages at once, and the first verse was the one everybody had, newcomer and native alike, and even before she could follow the drum-speech she knew what it said, because the shape of it said it:

Down here, down here, the dark keeps what the light lets fall. It asks no name, it holds no court, it counts no tribute, keeps no wall. What the light throws away, the dark picks up. Down here. Down here.

A defiant lullaby, a beggars' anthem, a national hymn for a nation composed entirely of what other nations had discarded. New verses grew on it the way weed grew on a wreck: the exiles had one, about names left at the shelf-edge; the disfigured had one, about eyes that fit the dark better than the light had ever fit them; even the eels had one, she was assured, though what it concerned no one had ever established. You did not learn the song. You were, eventually, included in it, verse by verse, as the deep decided which of its sorrows were yours to carry, and the verses came to her over the years the way trust comes, irregularly, sideways, after supper.

But there was one verse nobody sang in the open field.

She heard it entire only in her fifth year, in the brooding-caves of the oldest families, at the turning of the long dark season, at what she had come to understand was the deep's one holy occasion — not a festival; the opposite of a festival; a night for keeping rather than spending — and she heard it because the Matriarch of the plated peoples sent for her, which had never happened, and the sending-for was itself the news: the one who writes is wanted at the keeping, to hear.

The Matriarch was old the way the smokers were old. Her plates had gone the gray-white of barnacled stone, and her eyes had sunk so deep in the armor that looking into them was looking down two wells, and she lay in the great cave among her line — four generations, plated shoulder to plated shoulder, filling the dark like a field of boulders — and around and above them hung the rest of the old deep, the eyeless and the cathedral-things and the long dim shapes that never came to hearings, all of them still, all of them settled into that lowering, aimed-at-nothing attention Ursula had noticed in her first season and learned not to ask about.

And they sang the hidden verse, and it was not defiant at all, and that was what no one in the upper fields could be trusted with. It was a lament, and it was in the oldest drum-speech, and the Matriarch spoke it for her afterward, plainly, one slab of sentence per breath, the way the Brooder had argued its boundary, translating not the words but the account:

"We were not born down here," the Matriarch said. "You knew that."

"I knew it," Ursula said. "The way I know things I've been told."

"Then be told properly." The wells of the eyes held her. "We are the peoples of the shelf. The cold roads. The long grounds. My grandmother's grandmothers grazed water so far north of your reef that your reef's light was a story the currents told, and the roads ran through our grounds the way your streets ran through your city — the whale-roads south in the bearing season, the turtle-roads east to the warm sands and home, the cold rivers in the sea that carried the feed, and the feed carried everything. Every people on the shelf kept its stretch of road. That was what a people was. You kept the road, and the road kept you."

She stopped. In the dark of the cave, the four generations of her line made, all together, a low sound that was not a word.

"Then the water went wrong," the Matriarch said. "Slowly, the way the tide comes in over flats — no wave, no day you could name, just the warm creeping north along the shelf, season on season, a warmth with no sun in it and no summer behind it, a warmth that came up from the stone of your reef and spread like oil on water. The feed follows the cold; the cold drew back; the roads emptied. Not closed — emptied. A road is not a place, writer. A road is a promise: that the feed will be there when you arrive, because it has always been there, because the water has always been the water. The warm broke the promise. The bearing-grounds starved in a generation. The peoples of the shelf went to the reef-king in the old way, with the old courtesies, and asked him to take his warmth back into his walls where it was wanted, and were told" — the slab of the sentence came down — "that the sea is large, and the cold is larger, and a people that cannot hold its grounds should seek grounds it can hold."

"And then it was a war," Ursula said, because someone had to say it, and it was not going to be the cave.

"Then it was a war." The Matriarch's plates shifted, the sound of a cliff considering. "The Warm War, we called it, because we are plain people and we name a thing for its cause; I am told the reef does not call it anything at all. It was not long, as your records would count it. What is a war between roads and a wall? He came to the shelf himself, with the fork. We were many, and old, and knew the water as he has never—" she stopped, and chose a smaller sentence. "The trench where my mother's people massed is still there. It is shaped like a shell opened by a knife. Nothing has grown in it in a hundred and forty years. When it was done, the warm water stood over every ground we had ever held, and the feed was gone north beyond reach of the young and the old, and the roads were dead, and the king's writ ran to the edge of the shelf. So the peoples went where the writ did not run and the warm could not follow." The deep-set eyes did not blink; she had never seen them blink. "Down."

"Over the edge."

"Down the walls of the world, road by road, family by family, into the dark that asks no name. The half of us the cold-fall didn't take. We were the first, writer. Before the exiles, before the maimed, before your Verge friend's people, before you. The dark took us in when the light had eaten our roads, and we have kept its manners ever since, and that" — the Matriarch settled, the whole cliff of her, down into the cave floor, the lowering, the aimed-at-nothing stillness, and for the first time Ursula understood what the gesture was aimed at — "is why the verse is not sung in the open field. Grief is a ground too. We hold it."

Ursula was quiet a long time. Then she said: "Will you say it once more, slowly? I want to set it down."

And she added the Warm War to the wall, in the plain hand, from the Matriarch's mouth, with the empty trench and the emptied roads and the courtesies and the answer, and beneath it, because the record was the record: This war does not appear in the songs of Atlantica. I was educated in those songs for twenty-five years. I am the first person of my city to hear its name.

She was thirty by then, and the household had stopped being strange even to her.

It had assembled itself the way the deep assembled everything, sideways, and by her seventh year it had the settled shape it would keep: the eels at their stations; the Verge digger — Hurst, she called him now, for the sound his plates made settling, and he had let the name stand — at the heavy end of every task; and old Vess in the side-fault, the blind apothecary, who had arrived one season to trade tinctures for a hearing and never entirely left.

Vess was of the eyeless kind, older than the Matriarch by her own account and disbelieved by everyone, and she knew the deep's pharmacy the way Ursula knew the forty-one articles — which polyp's sting slowed a heart and which sped it, which weed sealed a wound in cold water, what the smokers' silver smoke left on the stone that would eat rot out of a fin, how to render the pale grazers down to a paste that let a warm-water body sit the cold margin an hour past its number. She taught the way Odell had taught, mercilessly, by asking; a season of and what will that do, and how do you know, and what did you fail to weigh; and Ursula, who had assumed her hands were only good for a pen, learned the other trade of her exile in the side-fault by lamp-worm light, and mixed, and ground, and titrated, and entered all of it on the wall, doses and errors both, because Vess could not write and had been waiting eighty years, she said, for the dark to send her a clerk.

It was Vess who solved the ink.

Ursula had been trying for years — soot-slurries, weed-gums, everything the cavern offered; the wall was permanent, but the wall could not travel, and a registry that cannot travel serves only those who can reach it. It was Vess who took her down to the cold seeps where the lamp-jellies bred and showed her how their light could be fixed — rendered, salted with smoker-silver, sealed in shell — into an ink that was not merely black but lit, faintly, from inside, so that a line written in it glowed on the page like a vent-seam in the dark, and could be read in lightless water by lightless eyes, which — Vess pointed out, tapping the first jar with one blind feeler, in the tone of a woman correcting two hundred years of a profession's oversight — the reef's cuttle-black never could. "Your city wrote for the sighted in the sunlight," she said. "You will be writing for everyone else. Make the words carry their own lamp."

The first contract written in jelly-ink on cured skin went up the shelf-slope that season to a boundary hearing in the mid-dark, and came back marked, and the marks glowed, and Ursula hung it on the wall of the cavern and looked at it a long time: a deal that could travel. Words that carried their own lamp. She did not know — how could she have known; even the ink did not know what it was rehearsing — she only knew that something in the sight of it sat in her chest like the click of a key turning over, one ward of many, in a lock she had not found yet.

And the news came down, all those years, and the news was the shadow.

It came the way everything came, sideways: with the traders who worked the shelf-slope, with the empty stones, and — more every year; this was itself the news, though it took her years to read it — with the new arrivals. The Abyss had always received a trickle of the fallen. Around her tenth year the trickle became a current. Kelp-villagers, whole families of them, rope-scarred, carrying nothing, with the particular stunned courtesy of people who had been respectable the season before last. Shellfish-tenders from beds that were sand now, two fat years and then the sand, exactly as a crab on a long table had once predicted to a king. Cold-current people, taxed in labor, whose strong had gone up the water to work the crown's harvests and come back at the season's end older than the season should have made them, or not come back. Verge folk — more of Hurst's scattered kin among them — moved off ground after ground as the reviews concluded and the charters were clarified, nine generations of tenure clarified away in a clerk's afternoon.

She heard each arrival's account the way she heard a party to a dispute, and entered it, and the wall grew its third section, the one she came to think of as the shadow-ledger: not disputes, arrivals. Where from. What held, before. What was taken, and under what heading, and what the heading actually meant. Kelp quotas set in the Harvest War's last year and never lowered since, cutting the forests faster than forests grow. Bed-leases harvested to the quick and sold before the third year. Labor-tribute assessed on villages by their population in their best remembered season, so that every death and every flight raised the burden on whoever stayed. Column by column, arrival by arrival, ten years of it in the plain hand on the black glass, and the columns all summed the same direction, and the direction was up.

Up. Everything went up. The kelp went up, the shell went up, the pearl went up, the strong young went up, the seasons of labor went up; and gold came down the same water — she had copied the rolls herself at eighteen, she knew to the span what came down — and the gold stopped at the crown's own treasury and the crown's own terraces and the crown's own walls, and what came on past the last lamp of the third terrace was quotas, and reviews, and clarifications. She had lived twenty-five years inside the shine and ten years underneath it, and from underneath, at last, with the whole wall of it in front of her in her own hand, she could see the shape of the machine entire, and the shape was simple. It was the simplest shape there was. The golden reef was not beside the dying regions. The golden reef was made of the dying regions. The reef did not have a shadow. The reef was the shadow's other name, the bright side of one single object, and she had grown up on the lit face of it copying the instruments of the dark one, and every fair lease she had ever admired at her father's desk had been a leaf on a branch on a trunk with its roots in this.

She was a long time at the wall, the sleep she finally saw it. The household let her be. Flotsam hung in the warm fault; Jetsam held the mouth; somewhere down the fields the song was moving through the smokers, what the light throws away, the dark picks up.

And the dark is welcome to it, said the deep's whole manner, the manner she had been learning for ten years, the settled grammar of the fallen. We do not go back up. The dark keeps what the light lets fall; that is the deal; that has always been the deal.

She looked at the shadow-ledger. Ten years of arrivals. Ten years of receiving the fallen at the bottom of the walls of the world, feeding them, hearing them, entering what was taken from them — and every column summing upward toward a machine that would go on dropping people over the edge exactly as fast as it could clarify their ground out from under them, forever, because nothing that fell ever went back up to stand where the falling started. The Abyss caught. It was magnificent at catching; it had caught her. But a catcher at the bottom of a cliff is not an answer to the cliff.

A fair deal has two sides you can weigh. And the whole long machine of the regions — quota and lease and labor and review — was ten thousand deals with only one side to them, ten thousand takings wearing deals' clothing, and there was not one notary standing in all that falling water, and she was the last one her father had made, and she was sitting in a hole in the ground with the finest registry in the ocean, serving the already-fallen.

Ursula turned from the wall. The eels' heads came up together, a door opening.

"We're going up," she said.

## 14

She went up the walls of the world with two eels and a satchel, and the satchel held everything she owned: three jars of the glowing ink, a roll of cured skins, two ground spines, Vess's travel-paste in a shell box, a key that opened nothing, and two stones that weighed sweets.

No magic. That must be said plainly, because of everything that came after: the woman who went up the shelf-slope in her thirty-fifth year had no power in her of any kind. She could not bind a body or brighten a page. Her ink glowed because jellyfish had died to make it, and her deals held because she drafted them level, and when they broke — they broke. She was a copyist with a satchel, unarmed as a word, and she walked the dying regions for two years on nothing but her parents' training, and the regions, which had learned to expect nothing from anybody, took a season even to understand what she was offering.

What she was offering was the third stone.

"You're not a party to it," the headwoman of the first village said, slow, working the strangeness of it. "You don't take a share. You don't hold the ground. You just — write it level, and stand there."

"And stay standing there," Ursula said. "That's the whole office. Someone who was present at the level, and doesn't move, and can be pointed to. You've been making deals without one for a hundred years. How have they held?"

The headwoman looked out at her village — the racks a quarter full, the boats a third crewed, the kelp-shadow thin over water that had been forest when her mother tended it — and made the sound the regions made in place of the answer everyone knew.

So it began. A boundary first — two shell-tending families, a bed split by a grandfather two generations back with a wave of the arm, the wave differently remembered ever since; she walked the bed with both families for a day, found the seam in the shell-growth where the old tending had actually changed hands, and wrote it, and both marked, and the glowing line hung in the headwoman's cave where anyone could come and see level with their own eyes. Then a debt: a boat-lender and a fisher, the debt grown teeth through three bad seasons, the lender within his written rights to take the boat and the fisher's whole standing with it; she sat between them through a long evening and rebuilt it — the boat stays working, for who starves fastest when a boat stops?; the debt takes a fixed span of the catch, not a share, so a bad season lengthens the term instead of deepening the hole; the lender's forbearance buys him first call on the good season. Level. Marked. Held.

Then a harvest shared: two kelp villages, one with standing weed and no hands, one with hands and a stripped shadow — the obvious deal, the deal a child could see, undone for a decade because the last such deal had been broken and the breaking was still owed. She drafted it with the breaking inside it — the old grievance written out plainly in the preamble, priced, and set off against a first-season margin — because her father had taught her that the deal that pretends the past didn't happen is signed by nobody, and her mother had taught her that any debt can be carried if it is only once honestly weighed. Both villages marked it. Hands went into the standing weed for the first time in ten years, and the racks filled, and that winter no one in either village went into the lean month short.

That one held. That one holds yet.

And word moved through the regions the way it had moved through the deep, sideways, faster than she traveled: there's a woman walking the water who writes things level. She takes a meal and a bed and nothing else. She has no house behind her and no goad in her hand — nothing but her word — and the things she writes hold.

Mostly. Mostly, they held.

The one that broke, broke in the cold-current fringe, in her second year, and it was the smallest deal she wrote on the whole passage, and she entered it afterward word for word, because the record was the record, and this belonged in it more than anything that held.

Two households of a current-village, kin twice over, and a shared claim on a mussel-ground: the strong house worked it, the weak one — an old man, his widowed daughter, her children — held the elder right. She wrote it the obvious level: the strong house takes the labor and six spans in ten; the elder house takes four for the right and the tending of the drying-racks, which was work the old man could still do. Both marked it, kin smiling at kin, her glowing line between them. It was the kind of deal that had held forty times behind her.

What she had not weighed was the patrol.

The crown's assessors came through the fringe that autumn, early, counting for the labor-tribute, and the strong house's headman panicked at his own margins — a man with sons of levy age does arithmetic fast and badly — and took the whole harvest up the water to the tribute-station to buy his sons clear, the elder house's four spans with it. No violence, no denial even; he stood in front of her line hanging in the meeting-cave and said, not meeting anyone's eyes, that he would make it good in the spring, and everyone knew the spring could not be made good in advance, and there was nothing behind the words on the skin but the words on the skin. She was three villages north when it happened. By the time the empty stone reached her — the fringe had taken up her customs; that was the bitter joke of it, the stone came properly — the lean month was half spent, and the old man, who had been trading his own share of the meals to the children all along in the way of grandfathers everywhere, which no clause she wrote could reach, had lain down on the drying-racks he tended and not gotten up.

No one called it her fault. That must also be said plainly: not the daughter, not the village, not even the reneging headman, who wept at the burying like a man broken across the middle. The fringe had buried its old in the lean month for a hundred years; one more was weather. They gathered at the burying and sang — the regions' burying-song, which she was hearing by then for the fortieth time — and the daughter pressed Ursula's hands and thanked her, thanked her, for the four spans that had fed the children as long as they had, and Ursula stood in the cold current in the middle of all that terrible absolution and did the accounting alone, because she was the only one present with the training to do it.

The deal was level. The drafting was good. And the old man was dead of it anyway, because level is a property of the page, and the page had nothing behind it — no court, no crown, no cost to breaking that a frightened man weighs in the moment of breaking. Her father's whole office had rested on a state; the state stood behind the seal; the seal, she now knew better than anyone living, stood on a moved line and two unentered graves. But it had stood behind the seal. You could hate the crown — she hated it with a completeness that no longer even had heat in it — and still read the lesson written on the drying-racks in a hand plainer than her own: a word with nothing behind it is a candle in open water. It is true. It is bright. It buys the dark exactly one hour, and then the current has it, and the ones who trusted the light are worse off than if it had never been lit, because they stood still in the dark while it burned.

Nothing but my word, she had said all up the regions, and meant it as her whole strength.

She wrote the old man's name on the skin of the broken contract, and the date of the burying, and carried it — carries it; it hung in the lair to the last night, glowing, among all the ones that held — and she went on north with the eels through the failing water, and in her chest, where the festival had once lived, something had begun to ask, in a voice that would be answered inside the year: then what would be enough? What could stand behind a word, that is not a crown? What weight is there in any water that a frightened man could not slip — what could hold a promise level after the promising, the way stone holds a road—

She did not know she was describing anything. The sea knew.

The Harvest War found her in the kelp country, the way the Warm War had found her in the brooding-caves: as a song she thought she knew, sung wrong.

She had grown up on the war the way she had grown up on the light. It was the second-oldest of the great songs, the war that fed Atlantica: the northern kelp lords, fat and treasonous, who had held back the harvest to starve the young reef into paying their price, and the king who broke them in a single season and set the forests free, so that the kelp came down the water to the terraces forever after, and no lord ever again stood between the city and its bread. She had sung it at eleven festivals before she could count the beats.

In the kelp villages they sang it too — the same song, the same tune; that was the thing that stopped her breath — but they sang it slow, in the burying-mode, and they had kept verses the reef had lost, or the reef had kept verses the villages never made; there was no way, ever again, for anyone to say which. In the villages' verses the kelp lords were still fat and still lords — no one wept for the lords — but the forests the king freed were freed the way the trench had been opened: with the fork. Whole stands of standing weed, forest that had shadowed the water since before the roads died, laid flat in an afternoon to break the lords' granaries, and the lords broken, truly, in a season, exactly as the bright verses said. And then the verse the reef did not sing, the one they gave her in an old woman's cave with the rain of the surface drumming faint through forty fathoms, four lines, in the plain register the regions kept for their exact griefs:

The lords are gone, the weed is free, the free weed goes to the crown. The quota came the year the war did end, and the forest did not stand back up.

Flattened forest does not reseed like meadow; the holdfasts crush, the ground sours; she knew that from her own survey year, clause by clause. The stands the war had laid down had come back thin or come back never, and into the gap between the song's promise and the water's fact had come the quotas — set in the war's last year, on the war's last good harvest, and never lowered since, assessed forever on forests that no longer existed against a number that no longer meant anything, and the villages had spent a hundred years cutting deeper into less to render a dead year's plenty to the crown. The war that fed Atlantica. It had. It did. She had eaten it all her childhood, and it was this: the same song, at two speeds, and only the slow one true.

The old woman who gave her the verse was called Calla, and she was the one the village brought its strangers to, because she was the oldest thing in it: born in the city itself, she said, two hundred years back and more, come out to the kelp country as a young teacher when the villages could still hire teachers — and Ursula, hearing the cadence of it, felt the world tilt gently, because the cadence was her mother's. A settler of the first water. One of Them That Came Down, as the villages said. They sat up late in the little cave, the rain drumming, the eels coiled at the entrance, and Calla fed her and questioned her — sideways, deftly; teachers do not retire — and it was the nearest thing to her mother's table in eleven years, and Ursula, who could not lie but had learned everywhere not to volunteer, found herself volunteering: the third terrace, the scriptorium, her parents' names, even — because Calla's eyes were old and dark and did not miss things — the shape, though not the matter, of why she could not go home.

"Scholars," Calla said. "Yes. You have it all over you. I taught with a woman once—" and she named a name that was not Ione, some colleague of a century past, and was off down the byways of a long memory, the city as the regions' old remembered it: the terraces going up, the good years, the teaching-halls. And Ursula, warm and fed and eleven years unguarded, asked the question she had asked her mother across a desk a lifetime ago — asked it easily, the way you hand someone a familiar tool:

"What was the city like when you were small? Before the water?"

And she watched it happen to a stranger.

The reach. The hand closing on nothing. Calla's face, mid-sentence, mid-warmth, going still — not troubled; that was the terrible part, the same part it had always been; untroubled, smoothed, like water closing over a dropped stone — and then the old voice saying, in the mild tone of a fact too obvious to examine, the sentence Ursula could have spoken along with her, word for word, from a desk on the third terrace:

"It was the city, child. It was there. It's always been there."

"But before—"

"We left the greed of men behind us," Calla said, simply, the way you cite scripture at a child, and patted her hand, and poured the tea-weed, and went on with the colleague of a century past; and the gap closed over its own edges as if it had never opened, and the rain drummed, and Ursula sat with her hand under the old warm hand and made no further sound, because eleven years ago she had watched her mother stand at this same closed door, and her mother had at least felt the door — had pressed two fingers to the bridge of her nose, had said it bothers me — and that, Ursula understood now, holding still in the little cave, had been rare; had been, perhaps, her mother; had been, perhaps, the beginning of everything that happened to her. The door was city-wide. It was regions-wide. Every soul of the first water carried it, terrace and fringe alike, closed on the same silence, and the whole hymn-singing ocean above and below her had agreed, without ever once being asked, that there was no door there at all.

She entered it that night on a cured skin, by ink-light, while Calla slept: Calla of the kelp country, born before the water. The same reach. The same closing. The same sentence, word for word, four hundred leagues from where my mother said it. It is not age. It is not grief. It is one wall, standing in every one of them, and it was built by something, and the builder is still tending it.

She looked at the last clause a long time. Then she let it stand, and capped the ink, and lay listening to the rain in the dark, twenty fathoms of water and a whole cosmology between her and the sky, and did not sleep for a long time.

The watching began in the north, in her last season on the passage, and for seven days she did not admit it.

## Part Three — The Witch's Bargain

The regions had loved the scrivener. The regions were afraid of what came back up the roads in her place, and she made herself learn that the way she learned everything, by entering it.

At the first village of the kelp country the children were called indoors before she had crossed the last stand of weed. She hung in the open water at the village edge — eight-armed, lit along every limb with the small drifting constellations of the deep, dark where she had been reef-pale — and waited, in plain sight, unmoving, the way you wait at a border with a matter, while the village looked at her through its doorways and decided what it was seeing. It took most of a day. What decided it was old Calla, who came out at dusk with a lamp she did not need and peered up at the shape the deep had sent, and said, in the dry cadence of a teacher taking attendance on a pupil grown past recognition:

"Well. You've been studying."

That was the sentence that let her back into the regions, and it traveled ahead of her afterward the way all Calla's sentences traveled, and it did more for her than any wonder she worked, because it gave the villages a category that was not monster. Studying, they could understand. The deep had schools the light knew nothing of; everyone had always said so; and the scrivener had gone down to them a woman and come back up — studied. Feared, yes. But the regions had a long-standing arrangement with fear. What they could not have survived was another power that wanted something from them, and it took one season of watching her to establish what the new shape wanted, which was what the old shape had wanted: a meal, a bed, and two marks on a level line.

The first contract of the ink was written in the third village of the kelp country, between Headman Brack and the family called Withy, over the beds at South Shadow, and she chose it with care, and she never pretended otherwise afterward. She had forty candles' worth of grief behind her and one grave, and she was carrying a new law in her body that no living creature had seen work, and she did not intend its first test to be a subtle one. She wanted a deal that was plainly level, between parties plainly unequal, one of whom would plainly cheat.

Brack was the kind the regions grew wherever the crown's weight came down through a single set of hands: the headman who met the quota, and skimmed the meeting of it. The Withys held the South Shadow beds by right so old it had no paper — nine generations of tending, the same claim she had heard argued on the Verge at eighteen — and Brack had been harvesting those beds for years under color of the village's tribute, taking the Withys' shell to fill the crown's baskets first and his own second, so that the family did the starving for the whole arrangement. It was, in miniature, the entire machine she had mapped on the cavern wall, and every village had one, and everyone knew, and nothing held him, because the only scale in the village hung in Brack's own shed.

She heard both sides for two days, in the open, in the meeting-cave, in front of the village — that was already strange to them; Brack's arrangements had never before had an audience — and then she weighed it aloud, plainly, her father's way. The beds were the Withys'; nine generations outweighed no paper. The quota was real; the crown's baskets did not care whose shell filled them; a headman who missed the count brought the assessors down on everyone, Withys included. So: the Withys lend their labor to the tribute harvest, a full season, every hand they had, Brack's beds and their own together — that was what he lacked, hands; his skimming had always been laziness before it was greed. Level. She read it out twice. She had not known, herself, what the watching would show. Ka'analoa had named the law and not the mechanism; the promise will live in the flesh of the promiser; she had imagined — she had deliberately imagined nothing. What happened was this, and she entered it on the wall of her lodging night by night, in the plain hand, a clinical record, because it was the first knowledge of its kind in the world and she was the only one placed to take it down:

@Fourth day. He went out to his own beds with the morning crews and could not lift the net. Not weakness — I spoke to two who saw. The hand closes on the haft and will not raise it. His arm obeys him in everything else; he carried baskets; he pulled a mooring. The net, for the shell he owes against, his hand declines. He is telling the village it is a strain from the harvest.

@Sixth day. The path to the tribute-stair passes the Withys' door. There is no other path; the village is built on a seam. His feet will not carry him past their door. Again not weakness — witnesses say he walks to within sight of the lintel and stops, like a boat coming to the end of its line; stands; turns back. He has sent his sister to the stair with the headman's tokens twice. He is telling the village it is his knees.

@Ninth day. He came to me. Furious, frightened, scratching the heel of his marking hand against the door-post like a man with fever — there is an itch, he says, under the skin, where he touched the page. It began as nothing. It grows a little every day he withholds. It does not break the skin and does not show and does not stop, and rubbing does not reach it, because — I did not tell him this — it is not in the skin. It is in the keeping. He demanded I lift the contract. I told him the truth: that I cannot, that no power in any realm unwrites it, that it is not punishing him and will not stop at punishment, because it is not anger. It is just the deal, still there. The deal does not go away because it is inconvenient. That is the entire nature of a deal. He called me a witch and several things older. I wrote it down.

@Sixteenth day. He paid.

He paid the way such men pay, all at once, gracelessly, in front of everyone, hauling the Withys' spans to their door with his own restored hands and dumping the baskets like a man extinguishing a fire; and the itch stopped that hour — she confirmed it with him later, in the doorway of his shed, the one civil conversation they ever had — stopped utterly, like a bell taken by the throat, and the net rose for him the next morning, and his feet owned the whole village again, and nothing whatever was wrong with Headman Brack, who had kept his word.

"You did that," he said to her in the doorway. Not accusing, by then. Establishing. Headmen live by knowing where power sits.

"I did nothing," Ursula said. "I watched, same as the village. You signed a level deal and then you tried to stand in two places — inside it for the harvest and outside it for the paying. There's no outside. That's all the ink is: a deal with no outside. You want someone to blame for your hard month, find the man who signed something he meant to break." She settled her arms, lights moving slow. "You met him at the racks."

@· · ·

Two versions of that season went out along the water, and she heard them both come back to her within the year, changed by travel, the way she had once watched a lament go door to door.

The Withys' version was short, and the family told it kneeling — she could not break them of it, that first year, the kneeling; the old grandmother had pressed the witch's hands to her forehead at the racks in front of everyone with the spans sitting paid at her door, and wept, and called down on her the blessing of every small god the kelp country kept, and the story the family told was the story of the level line: there is a woman in the water now, and if she writes your due on a page, your due arrives. It cannot be skimmed. It cannot be regretted. The strong keep faith with the weak down here now, whether the strong like it or not. Go to her. She weighs true.

Brack's people told the other one. He had people — headmen always have people, cousins at the tribute-stair, friends among the assessors, all the small keepers of the machine whose skimming rests on his — and their version had no baskets in it and no nine generations. Their version was about the thing itself: there is a witch out of the deep in the kelp country, and she binds folk with a glowing ink that is part of her body, and a man who crossed her could not lift his own net, could not walk his own village, was eaten from inside by an itch no healer could find, until he did her will. Sixteen days. A headman, mind — not some tenant. If it can close a headman's hand, whose hand can it not close?

Both versions were true. That was the discovery of her fortieth year, the one she circled back to on the wall again and again as the two stories spread and braided and went out past the kelp country into the shell roads and the cold current and, eventually, up: you could not tell the blessing without telling the terror, because they were the same fact wearing two needs. The desperate heard the strong cannot cheat you anymore and came to her in growing lines. The strong heard your strength has stopped working and began, for the first time in a hundred years, to be afraid of something that swam below them. She had built the thing she had described to a god at a black stone — the keeping, in the flesh, the road that holds — and it had taken one season and one greedy man to teach her what she should have known from her own childhood among the songs: that a scale no one can move is a wonder to whoever the tilt has been robbing, and an outrage to whoever the tilt was carrying, and that the second party writes to the capital.

She entered that, too. Then she went on with the circuit, because the empty stones were arriving faster now, six and eight behind her on the road, matters queued at every village.

Of what the season had cost her, the record says only this, and it says it without understanding it, low on the wall of a lodging in the kelp country, in the plain hand, between a dosage note of Vess's and the terms of a boundary:

@The Withy grandmother wept on my hands at the racks. Note: I remember that this, from a stranger, in my mother's cadence, would once have been more than I could stand steady under. I stood entirely steady. Tired, I think. It has been a long season.

It had been. And the ink rose in her like breath, in any quantity she needed, exactly as promised, and asked for nothing she could feel leaving.

## 17

The seventh daughter was born at the turn of the cold season, in the last hour before light, which the midwives said was a lucky hour, as they had said of six different hours before.

Triton waited in the long corridor outside the birthing rooms, because that was where kings waited; he had learned the post at the first birth and kept it at all seven, hanging in the corridor's warm gloom with the trident in his hand while the lamps burned low and the sounds came and went behind the coral wall. The palace slept around him in rings. Somewhere below, six girls who were supposed to be asleep were awake in one bed, waiting for word, and he knew it, and had not sent anyone to scatter them, and this was the sort of thing he knew how to do with his kingship now: spend it in small permissions, silently, where asking and granting never had to put on their clothes.

He was alone in the corridor for most of the middle watch, and at some point, without deciding to, he counted.

He did it on his fingers, against the haft, the way his—the way men counted in the fish-market of a city that no longer existed in any mind but his. Attina. Alana. Adella. Aquata. Arista. Andrina. Six fingers, six girls, six times in this corridor; and the seventh coming now, behind the wall, in the lucky hour; and his thumb stood up off the haft to take the seventh count, and stopped there, raised, waiting, and he looked at it.

Seven daughters. No son.

He was not a fool about it. He had been a fishmonger's boy; he knew how the catch ran; seven of one thing was the sea being the sea. And he had built the succession laws himself, with two scholars whose names he did not think in this corridor, and a daughter could hold Atlantica as well as any son — better, if the daughter was Attina. The thought was not about thrones. It was older and lower than thrones, and it had been rising in him at each of these vigils a little nearer the surface, and this time, in the low lamps, with his thumb standing off the haft, it broke water entire:

@A dynasty dependent on the gift.

The words came through him in a voice that was not quite his own thinking — measured, dry, like a term read into a contract — and he stood very still and asked himself, as a man asks about a stone in his boot, where he had picked them up. Not from Sebastian. Not from the scholars. Not from any petition or song; he would have remembered; his memory was the one whole thing in the ocean. The words had simply been in him, the way the calm was in the water at the trading stone, arriving without weather; and he could no more remember first hearing them than he could remember the trident being heavy. He flexed his hand on the haft. It was the only sentence in two hundred years he could not source, and it recurred, and it was always true when it came, and he had never once said it aloud.

No son. Seven times no son. The sea being the sea.

He put the thought where he put things, which was nowhere, which was a room in him with no wall, and the thought went on standing in it, patiently, the way everything stood in that room, and down the corridor the door opened.

@· · ·

The child was small, and loud out of all proportion to it, and had been born, the chief midwife said, in the caul and in a hurry, which meant a traveler.

He came into the warm bright room with the trident still in his hand because it had not occurred to him to set it anywhere, and the room arranged itself around his coming the way rooms did — the midwives drawing back along the walls, the exhausted queen half-risen against her pillows with her ladies at her shoulders — and the chief midwife stood in the middle of it all with the wrapped bundle against her, jigging it gently, and the small furious voice went on telling the whole palace exactly what it thought of arrival.

He wanted to hold her.

The want was immediate and total, the same flood he had stood in six times before — and he stood in it, in the middle of the room, with a god's fork in his fist, and did what he had done six times before, which was nothing. The asking was one word. May — even a king says may in a birthing room; his own—men in the old market had said it, big rough men gone soft in doorways: may I hold him? may I see? The word stood in his mouth. His mouth did not say it. His hand, his free hand, the strong asking hand of a boy who had once begged rain from an empty sky, hung at his side and did not so much as turn its palm; and the room, which could not give what was not asked and could not refuse what was not asked and could not breathe until the standing stopped, made its accommodation with the stillness of the king the way the whole ocean had learned to: the chief midwife, who had done this six times, crossed to him as if it were the ordinary way of things, and set the bundle in the crook of his empty arm herself, unbidden, saying comfortable nothing-words to cover the transaction, and the room breathed.

She had done it kindly. That was the stone in it. Everyone was always so kind about the thing he could no longer do, and none of them knew they were doing it, and he could never tell a living soul what the kindness cost, or what it covered, or that the king of Atlantica had stood in seven birthing rooms with his arm making no shape at all, wanting, in the silence where the one word used to live.

The seventh daughter stopped crying.

She did it all at once, mid-breath, in the crook of his arm, the way the sea flattens at the stone, and opened her eyes, which newborns of the reef mostly declined to do for days, and looked at him — at the lamps, the midwives said later; newborns look at lamps — looked at him, with an unfocused gray-green attention, and gripped the finger he gave her with her whole minnow hand, and held it, and he stood there held, in the lucky hour, and was, for the length of that grip, only what he looked like: a father, in a warm room, at the start of a life.

"Ariel," the queen said, from the pillows, in the tone of a woman who has been keeping a name a long time. "For the air in her voice. Listen to her. She came in shouting like a gull."

Like a gull. Nobody in the room had ever heard a gull. The word survived in old rhymes, a sea-bird of the songs' world, the world of the greed of men; the queen was quoting a nursery verse three centuries dead and did not know it, and Triton, who knew exactly what a gull sounded like over a fish-market at dawn, looked down at the small gray-green attention in the crook of his arm and heard, all unasked, the harbor: the creak and slap, the crying of the birds, the smell of the stalls opening. He let none of it reach his face. It was the deepest-buried skill he had.

"Ariel," the king agreed, and the name went out from the room in the way of royal names, along the corridor where six sisters exploded off a shared bed, down through the waking rings of the palace, out into the lamplit water of a city that would sing it, one day, in ways none of them in that warm room could have borne to be told.

@· · ·

The old courtier found him at mid-morning in the gallery, where he was being shown things — the gifts were already arriving, terrace by terrace, the seventh wave of them in forty years and the ritual worn smooth — and she was the widow of a man he had valued, first-terrace back to the founding, herself born a generation after the water, sharp at eighty as most were at forty. She made her courtesies over the gift-tables and stood beside him at the long window a while, in the license of the old, watching the light come down through the shallows onto the city, and said, in the drifting way of gallery talk:

"Seven princesses. My grandmother would have called that a blessed house and spat behind her hand, Majesty — she kept every old sign, my grandmother. First-water, you know. She used to say things were different once. Atlantis, she meant. Different in her girlhood."

"Different how."

He heard it leave him: too quick, too flat, the interrogative fallen off the end. The wrong voice for a gallery — the voice from the other room in him, where the sentence with no source stood. The courtier did not notice, or was too old and too bred to show noticing; but he had noticed, and stood in the noticing the way a man stands very still on ice, listening to it.

"Oh —" the old lady moved her fan, tracing it, and he watched her go back down her grandmother's years, back and back, toward the water's edge, and he waited at the rail of it, as he always waited, for what would come up. "The temples were empty, she'd say. That's all, Majesty. Empty temples, and the priests gone off, and the gold everywhere, and everyone so busy — she made it sound quite dreadful and rather exciting. And then the reckoning, of course, and the King's mercy" — a small bow of the head, the formula given to him as it had been given ten thousand times, the King's mercy, his own drowning handed back to him as a courtesy — "and after that, she'd say, things were as they should be. She never would say more than that. I used to press her — children are ghouls, Majesty, I wanted the reckoning, the fire, all of it — and she'd only pat me and say the wrongness was left behind us where it belonged." The fan moved. "Left behind us. As the song says. I suppose that is the song, or the song is her; one can't tell at my age which one learned it from which."

"No," Triton said. "One can't."

His shoulders had come down. He noted it the way he noted the warm line moving in the east shallows — a reading on an instrument he had stopped being able to find in himself, registering only in the body: the settling, the specific unclenching that came every time, every single time in two hundred years, when he lowered a line into one of the old ones and drew it back up with nothing on it. Empty temples. Gold. A reckoning. Mercy. The approved sediment, the same five spans of bottom every sounding found, in courtiers and kelp-wives and his own queen's late mother alike. The hole had held again. The city stood on water that stood on a hole, and the hole held, it always held, and he had not made it and had never once, in two hundred years, stopped checking it — his one inspection that appeared in no ledger, conducted in galleries and at festivals, in kind questions to the very old, and what was it like in your grandmother's day — the king walking his deepest wall, alone, forever, tapping it.

The old courtier had drifted to the gift-tables and was praising a christening-shell from the third terrace. Triton stayed at the window. Below him the city shone in the young light, and the light came down through the shallows in bars, and he became aware that he was rubbing, slowly, with his free hand, the heel of the hand that held the trident — an itch, almost, under the skin where the haft crossed the palm; not an itch; nothing; gone when he attended to it. Old hands. The sea being the sea.

@· · ·

They brought Ariel to him again at evening, in the quiet, and he held her at the window to show her the lamps coming up, because he had done that with each of them, and it had never once been dutiful, and it was the nearest thing he had to prayer.

She was awake again — a traveler, the midwife had said; she kept traveler's hours all her life — and the gray of the morning's gaze had begun already to settle toward its lasting color, and at the window, in the lamplight coming up off the whole bright bowl of the city, the king looked into his seventh daughter's eyes and went, without warning, through the floor of his own heart.

Sea-green. Sea-green with the light in them at the outer ring, that exact green, the green of clear water over pale sand at midday with the sun standing in it — he knew those eyes. He had known them before any palace and any pool; they had looked at him across a workshop, over a scaling-knife, through steam off a morning kettle; they had crinkled at their corners at some joke of his that history had not kept; they had watched for him from a doorway at dusk, above the lamps of the—above lamps. Whose doorway. Whose kettle. He reached — the king of memory, the one whole mind in the ocean, keeper of the harbor and the gull-cry and the smell of the stalls, reached into his own unbreached hoard for the face, the face around the eyes, her face —

— and found the eyes only. Green, warm, crinkled at their corners, hanging in a doorway of dimness his own long years had quietly eaten, like a lamp burning in a window of a house gone dark.

The trident had kept him whole, and whole was two hundred and thirty years long now, and no vessel is without a floor. Nothing had taken his mother from him. He had simply outlived his own keeping, the ordinary way, the mortal way, the way of every creature the trident had never touched; and of all the lessons the sea had set him in a long schooling, none had ever come in so quiet: that what the gods spare, time collects; that his hoard too was a hole, filling at its own patient rate; and that he could no longer tell anyone — there was no one left in any water to tell — whether the eyes now blinking up at him out of his seventh daughter's face were his mother's, or only his grief's, wearing green.

He stayed at the window a long time, holding the child, checking, and could not say what it was he was checking, and the lamps of Atlantica came up ring below ring below ring, all the way down to the dark.
